# How can I tell if my dog is unwell?

Look for changes from your dog's normal behaviour.
Dogs rarely show pain or illness openly, and owners tend to notice the obvious signs, such as limping, but miss the subtle ones.
Many behaviour changes that look like a training or temperament problem turn out to have a medical cause.

## Which signs of pain do owners miss?

The subtle ones.

- In an online survey of 530 dog owners and 117 people without dogs, both groups readily linked [limping, playing less and a change in personality](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0344512) to pain.
- For a dog whose pain showed only as **following family members around** and **restlessness at night**, owners were no better than non-owners at recognising pain.
- Some owners read signs such as turning away or freezing as stress rather than pain.

After surgery, owners of 51 dogs filmed them at home for a week.
The changes owners noticed most were in [walking (72%), playing with toys (70%) and playing with the owner (69%)](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0345418); dogs also explored and ate less.
But the owners' estimates of their dog's pain did not match the estimates of vets who watched the same videos.

## Signs that may mean pain

These come from the pain scales vets use and from the studies above.
None of them proves pain on its own; what matters is a change from what is normal for your dog.

- **Moving differently:** limping, stiffness, reluctance to jump, climb stairs or get up.
- **Doing less:** playing less, exploring less, walking more slowly.
- **Changes in mood:** a change in personality, restlessness, following you around, turning away, freezing.
- **Reacting to touch:** flinching, looking at or licking one spot, growling or snapping when handled.
- **Changes in eating and sleeping:** eating less, restlessness at night.

Vets score pain after surgery or injury with scales such as the short Glasgow scale, which rates [vocalising, attention to the wound, movement, response to touch, demeanour and posture](https://doi.org/10.1017/S096272860003178X); a score of 6 or more out of 24 means the dog needs more pain relief.
Even trained staff disagree: when 20 assessors scored videos of 45 dogs, [overall agreement was poor](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani14162310).
A review of pain scales found those for dogs [less well tested than those for cats](https://doi.org/10.1093/jvimsj/aalaf062), and there is no validated scale of facial expressions of pain for dogs.

For long-term pain, such as arthritis, owners answer questionnaires about how pain affects [activity, enjoyment of life, rising, walking, running and climbing stairs](https://doi.org/10.2460/ajvr.68.6.631).

Pain from inside the body is harder still to spot.
The "prayer position", front end down and rear end up, is often linked to belly pain, but in dogs with pancreatitis it [appears only in severe cases](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2025.1614403).

## Limping

Limping is the sign owners notice most easily, but its cause needs a vet.
Arthritis is a common cause in older dogs: in a year, [about 2.5% of dogs](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-23940-z) at UK vet practices were diagnosed with it, more often in older and heavier dogs and in breeds such as Labradors, and it affected them for about 11% of their lives.

I found no independent study of how well owners spot lameness, or of which kinds of limping need a vet urgently.

## Behaviour problems with a medical cause

Pain is often behind behaviour problems.

- Specialists reviewing their recent dog cases estimated that [about a third involved a painful condition](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani10020318), and in some caseloads nearly 80%.
  Hips, knees and backs were common, but so were painful gut and skin conditions.
- In 20 dogs referred for fear of noises, those with joint or muscle pain [developed the fear about 4 years later in life](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2018.00017), feared more places and avoided other dogs more.
  They improved once the pain was treated.
- Aggression that appears suddenly in a dog that was not aggressive before can be [a sign of pain](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani9121133).

So a new fear, new aggression or other sudden change in behaviour, especially in an adult dog, is a reason to see a vet before a trainer.

## Licking, chewing and scratching

All dogs lick and scratch, and I could not find an independent study that defines how much is too much.
The studies I found that measured licking or scratching with activity trackers were funded by the trackers' makers or by drug companies, which I don't cite.

Repeated licking or chewing of one spot, broken skin or hair loss are reasons to see a vet.

One unusual behaviour has a clear link to illness: **fly biting**, snapping at the air as if at invisible flies.
All [7 dogs with fly biting](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23729825/) given a full medical work-up in a Canadian study had a gut disease, and the behaviour stopped or improved in most once that was treated.

## Drinking more

Drinking more than about [90 to 100 ml per kg of body weight a day](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsap.70105) is treated as excessive; for a 10 kg dog, that is about a litre.
A dog that normally drinks little may be drinking too much at a lower volume, so a clear increase on its usual intake matters more than the number.
Drinking more [can have many causes](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsap.70105), including kidney disease, diabetes and hormone disorders, so see a vet.

## Eating grass or poo

Neither is usually a sign of illness:

- Grass eating is common and usually harmless: see [why do dogs eat grass?](why-do-dogs-eat-grass.md)
- In a large owner survey, [16% of dogs](https://doi.org/10.1002/vms3.92) were frequent poo eaters, seen at it six times or more.
  It was not linked to diet or age, the dogs targeted fresh stools, and products sold to stop it almost never worked.

## Older dogs: ageing or illness?

Some changes that owners put down to old age can be signs of **canine cognitive dysfunction**, a form of dementia, or of other illness.

- Owners often [put the signs down to normal ageing](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani16071117).
- In 15,019 dogs in the US Dog Aging Project, the odds of cognitive dysfunction [rose by 52% with each year of age](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-15837-9).
- In 84 older dogs, [accidents indoors](https://doi.org/10.1093/jvimsj/aalag208) were linked to age, more dilute urine and poorer scores on thinking tasks.
  So a vet check for both urinary and cognitive causes makes sense.

Whether exercise helps is a separate question: see [does exercise help keep an older dog's mind sharp?](does-exercise-help-an-older-dogs-mind.md)

## What isn't known

- How much licking or scratching is too much.
- How well owners spot limping, and which signs need a vet the same day.
- Whether activity trackers can catch illness early: the studies I found were run or paid for by the device makers or by drug companies.
- A validated way to read pain from a dog's face.

## What I take from this

- **Know your dog's normal.** Most signs of illness are changes, not specific behaviours.
- **Take subtle changes seriously:** following you around, restlessness at night or playing less can mean pain.
- **New behaviour problems need a vet first**, especially in an adult or older dog.
