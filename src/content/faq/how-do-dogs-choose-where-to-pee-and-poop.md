# How do dogs choose where to pee and poop?

Partly by smell, but less is known than you might think.
Dogs sniff before they pee, they can tell their own urine from other dogs', and adult males often pee over other dogs' marks.
But no study has tested whether a dog goes back to the same toilet spot because of old smells, because it remembers the place, or out of habit.

Several of the classic studies on scent marking are behind paywalls, and I could not read who funded them, so I don't cite them yet; they are listed on [papers I am looking for](../pages/papers-i-am-looking-for.md).

## Emptying the bladder or leaving a message?

A dog pees for two reasons: to empty its bladder, and to leave a scent mark for other dogs.
Marking shows up as many small pees on a walk, often against an upright object.

- **Adult males mark the most.** In 113 shelter dogs on 20-minute walks, [adult males peed far more often](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani13233649) than adult females, while young dogs of both sexes peed rarely and about equally.
  That points to marking as something dogs start doing as they mature.
- **Who walks the dog matters.** In 100 shelter dogs, males [peed more often with an unfamiliar woman](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani10040632) than with an unfamiliar man, and with a man they more often squatted like a young dog.
  Both sexes were more likely to poop with a woman walking them: about 74% of walks against 44%.

## Do dogs choose spots by smell?

For peeing, smell clearly matters.

- **Dogs sniff, then go.** In 286 observed walks, dogs that [sniffed more also peed and pooped more](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2010.03.007).
- **Dogs know their own urine.** The biologist Marc Bekoff moved patches of urine-soaked snow onto the path of his own dog over five winters.
  The dog [barely sniffed his own urine](<https://doi.org/10.1016/S0376-6357(01)00142-5>), and peed over it only 21% of the time, against 87% for other males' urine and 78% for females'.
  That is one dog, so it shows what a dog can do, not what all dogs do.
- **A test with 32 dogs agreed.** Dogs [sniffed other dogs' urine longer than their own](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beproc.2017.08.001), and sniffed their own longer when another smell had been added to it.
- **Dogs read who left the urine.** Forty pet Labradors were led past stakes smeared with urine from unfamiliar dogs or with water.
  They [sniffed the urine for about 5 seconds and the water for half a second](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.anbehav.2008.12.033), and sniffed urine from unneutered dogs longer than urine from neutered ones.
  Dogs that ranked lower in their own household sniffed longer than higher-ranking ones, which the authors read as checking out a possible threat.
- **Marking over marks.** Among free-ranging dogs in India, dogs that came across another dog's urine sometimes [peed over it](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00265-024-03547-3), mostly males over other males' urine, and more often for dogs from neighbouring groups.
  They peed over spots treated with plain water much less often.

### Why do dogs scratch the ground afterwards?

Dogs have scent glands in their paws, and the scratching may add scent to the mark.
Dogs do [sniff other dogs' paw scent longer](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-74784-5) than plain ground, so paw scent carries information.
But no study has shown that scratching after peeing or pooping actually leaves scent where it matters.

## Do dogs remember where they went, or only smell it?

Nobody knows: no study has tested it.
To find out, someone would have to remove or move the smell of a dog's usual toilet spot and see whether the dog still goes there.
Even in the snow study above, the urine was moved to where the dog would meet it; nobody checked whether he went back to the old spot.

What is known suggests dogs could use both:

- **Dogs remember places.** Dogs and wolves that [watched food being hidden](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0290547) found more of it than when they had not watched, and the authors conclude they did not simply rely on scent.
- **Dogs remember what they did.** Dogs could [recall a person's actions](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2016.09.057) after an hour, and [repeat their own earlier actions](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-67302-0) after up to an hour.
- **Dogs use both smell and memory to find their way.** Hunting dogs fitted with GPS collars [followed their own scent trail](https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.55080) back to their owner on 59% of returns, and took a new route on 33%.
- **Wolves choose visible spots.** An experiment with wild wolves suggests that leaving droppings [where they are easy to see](https://doi.org/10.1002/jez.70080) matters more to wolves than making the smell last; that is wolves, not dogs.

So the most likely answer is that dogs use smell, memory of places and plain habit together, but that is my inference, not something a study has shown.

## Grass, pavement or posts?

Dogs probably prefer grass and soil to hard surfaces, and urine collects at the foot of trees and posts; see [do dogs prefer to pee and poop on some surfaces over others?](do-dogs-prefer-some-surfaces-to-pee-and-poop-on.md)

## Do dogs line up north–south to poop?

Probably not in any way you would notice.

- A 2013 Czech study of 70 dogs reported that dogs [lined up north–south](https://doi.org/10.1186/1742-9994-10-80) when pooping and peeing, but only when the Earth's magnetic field was calm.
  The authors came up with the "calm field" idea after collecting the data, and one dog contributed a large share of the key observations.
- An independent study of 52 dogs in parks in Lyon found [no north–south alignment](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jveb.2021.11.005); the dogs lined up with the layout of each park instead.

## Why do dogs circle before pooping?

I found no study of circling.

## What this means for house-training

- **Advice helps.** In a trial with 113 families adopting from a shelter, five minutes of house-training advice raised the share who called their dog house-trained a month later [from 86% to 98%](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.231.4.558), although how often the dogs had accidents did not differ.
- **Stress can stop a dog going.** Dogs walked by an unfamiliar person, especially a man, marked and pooped less, so a nervous dog may need a familiar walker or more time.
- **Cleaning up smells is sensible but untested.** If dogs are drawn to the smell of earlier accidents, cleaning them thoroughly should help, but I found no study of whether enzyme cleaners or "go here" sprays change where dogs go.
