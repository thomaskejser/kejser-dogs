# Why do dogs make digging motions before they sleep or rest?

Many dogs scratch at their bed, a sofa cushion or the floor, turn round a few times, and then flop down.
The internet is full of confident explanations.
I could not find a single peer-reviewed study that tested any of them.

So this article is mostly about what we don't know, and about the nearby questions that have been studied.

## Has anyone studied it?

Not that I could find.
I looked for studies of dogs, wolves and other wild canids digging, pawing or circling before lying down, and found none in a peer-reviewed journal.

The only experiment I found on circling was published on a blog, not in a journal, so I leave it out.

## The usual explanations

Each of these is plausible, and each is untested.

### "It is a leftover from wolves digging dens"

**Untested, and the wolf evidence does not fit well.**

Wolves do dig dens, but for raising pups, not for everyday sleep.

- In the Rocky Mountains, [23 of 25 wolf dens](https://doi.org/10.22621/cfn.v122i1.543) were tunnels dug into hillsides, on average almost 3 m deep, ending in a chamber where the pups were born and nursed.
- In Wisconsin, wolves were seen [digging dens in autumn and winter](https://doi.org/10.5962/p.358223), months before their pups were born.
  The adults slept in beds in the open next to the den, not inside it.

So adult wolves seem to rest on the surface, and the digging we know about is for pups.
Whether wolves scrape or circle before lying down in those surface beds, I found no study that says.

### "It cools or warms the spot"

**Untested in dogs.**

Scraping away the top layer of soil might reach cooler ground in summer, or make a sheltered hollow in winter.
The closest study I found watched where free-ranging dogs in India chose to rest, over 6,047 sightings of 284 dogs across three years.

- Dogs resting [in groups lay on soil or sand](https://doi.org/10.1093/biolinnean/blae095) more often than dogs resting alone, which preferred concrete or tarmac.
  The authors suggest temperature could be one reason, but did not test it.
- Whether a spot was in shade made no difference to where the dogs rested.

The study recorded where dogs lay, not whether they dug or circled first.

### "It leaves the dog's scent on the bed"

**Untested.**

Dogs have sweat glands in their paw pads, and dogs [sniff other dogs' paw scent longer](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-74784-5) than plain ground, so paw scent carries information.
But nobody has tested whether pawing at a bed leaves scent there, or whether it matters to the dog if it does.
The same is true of scratching the ground after peeing, which I cover in [How do dogs choose where to pee and poop?](how-do-dogs-choose-where-to-pee-and-poop.md)

### "It flattens grass or bedding into a comfortable nest"

**Untested.**
It makes sense that a dog would turn and paw more on a lumpy surface than on a smooth one, but I found no peer-reviewed test.

### "It checks the spot for snakes or insects"

**Untested.**
The study of free-ranging dogs in India found they [preferred resting spots with few insects](https://doi.org/10.1093/biolinnean/blae095), but that is about choosing a spot, not checking it by pawing.

### "Dogs line up with the Earth's magnetic field"

**Untested for lying down.**
A 2013 Czech study says it watched dogs [resting, feeding and toileting](https://doi.org/10.1186/1742-9994-10-80), but reports results only for toileting, and a later study did not confirm even that; see [How do dogs choose where to pee and poop?](how-do-dogs-choose-where-to-pee-and-poop.md).

## The one case that is known: pregnant dogs nest

Here there is real evidence, though less than you might expect.

- **Nesting before giving birth.** As birth approaches, a pregnant dog [builds a nest or moves to a dark place](https://doi.org/10.1080/23144599.2019.1641899).
  A study of 30 mothers that were filmed giving birth on straw [scored "nest building"](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-10707-w) as scratching the bedding with the front paws or nudging it with the nose, much like the digging motion many dogs make at bedtime.
  It did not report how many of the mothers did it.
- **Searching for a den.** In India, [20 pregnant free-ranging dogs](https://doi.org/10.1038/srep32014) were followed before they gave birth.
  Each tried out between two and six places, three or four on average, before settling on the den where she had her pups, and often rejected a spot she had chosen earlier.
- **Little else is known.** A 2024 review calls research on dogs' maternal behaviour [scarce](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2024.1394201).
  How common nesting is, which hormones drive it in dogs, and whether it predicts how well a mother cares for her pups have not been measured.

Dogs that are not pregnant but go through a false pregnancy after their heat are also said to nest and "mother" toys.
The only study I found with numbers on this was part-funded by a company that sells treatment for false pregnancy, so I leave it out.

So if a female dog that is not spayed suddenly starts digging and tearing at her bedding, and is not just settling down for a nap, pregnancy or false pregnancy is worth considering, and worth a call to your vet.

## When digging at the bed may be a problem

I found no study of when bed-digging becomes a sign of a problem.
Common sense says to ask a vet if it is new, if your dog cannot seem to settle after it, or if it comes with other changes; [How can I tell if my dog is unwell?](how-can-i-tell-if-my-dog-is-unwell.md) lists signs that are worth acting on.

## What I take from this

- **Why dogs do it:** unknown.
  The usual explanations are reasonable guesses, not findings.
- **Wolves:** wolves dig dens for their pups.
  I found no study of what wolves do before lying down to rest.
- **Pregnant dogs:** they really do build nests before giving birth, scratching their bedding with their front paws.

For an ordinary dog pawing its blanket before a nap, I see no reason to worry, and no evidence that tells us why it does it.
