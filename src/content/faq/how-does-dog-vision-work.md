# How does dog vision work?

Dogs are famous for their noses, and their eyes get less attention, from owners and from scientists alike.
A 2017 review called dog vision ["substantially understudied"](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-017-1404-7), and most of the studies below tested fewer than ten dogs.
Still, enough is known to correct a few myths.

## What colours can dogs see?

**Solid:** dogs see colour, but fewer colours than we do.

Most people have three kinds of colour-sensing cells (cones) in the eye.
Dogs have two.

- In a classic experiment, [three dogs](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0952523800004430) learned to pick out a coloured light from two plain ones.
  Their eyes turned out to have one cone type most sensitive to violet-blue and one most sensitive to yellow-green.
  Their colour vision was very like that of a person with the commonest form of red–green colour blindness.
- The same study found that blue-green light looked grey to the dogs, and that they were poor at telling apart colours from green to red.
- Measuring the eye's electrical response in three dogs later [confirmed the two cone types](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0952523800003291), and found the same pair in three kinds of fox.

So a dog probably sees the world in shades of blue and yellow.

### Which colours stand out to a dog?

Based on those experiments, a [1995 review](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.1995.207.12.1623) splits the colours we see into three groups for a dog:

- **Violet and blue** probably look blue.
- **Green, yellow-green, yellow, orange and red** probably all look like shades of one colour, roughly yellow, differing mainly in how bright and how strong they look.
  A [1993 study of dog and fox eyes](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0952523800003291) puts browns in the same group, so anything bluish should stand out from browns, reds, yellows and greens.
  Dogs are least sensitive to red, so red probably looks dark.
- **Blue-green (turquoise)** probably looks grey or white, with no colour at all.

The strongest colours for a dog are at the two ends, blue and yellow; the colours in between look washed out.

That lets you predict which pairs are easy and which are hard.
These are predictions from the colour experiments, not things anyone has tested pair by pair:

| Probably hard to tell apart by colour | Probably easy to tell apart by colour |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Red and green                         | Blue and yellow                       |
| Red, orange and yellow                | Blue and green                        |
| A red or orange toy on green grass    | A blue toy on green grass             |
| A yellow toy on green grass           | A blue or yellow toy on grey concrete |
| Turquoise and grey or white           | Blue and red                          |
| Red and dark brown or black           | Yellow and turquoise                  |

Where two colours look alike to a dog, it can still tell them apart if one is much brighter, but it can't go by colour alone.
The review points out, for example, that a guide dog cannot read a traffic light by colour, and that training a dog to pick out a red object from a green one by colour is likely to fail.

If you want a toy your dog can spot on a lawn, blue is the best choice.
Orange, red and also yellow are all on the dog's "yellow" side, the same as the grass, and stand out only by brightness.

**Dogs do use colour, not just brightness.**

- In Russia, [8 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1098/rspb.2013.1356) were trained on pairs of cards that differed in both colour and brightness, such as dark yellow and light blue.
  When the researchers then swapped the brightness, the dogs went by colour in 81 of 90 trials.
- In Italy, [16 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.170869) watched a cat shape moving across a screen in colours taken from a human colour-blindness test.
  They looked at the cat that a colour-blind person can see, and looked much less at the one that only people with full colour vision can see.
- In India, [134 free-ranging dogs](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-024-01928-9) that had never been trained went for yellow objects more often than blue or grey ones.
  The authors could not control how bright the paints were, so this is a hint rather than proof.
- **Screens can mislead.** In Australia, [7 related Lagotto Romagnolos](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2019.01.004) trained on an old tube monitor told red from green as easily as black from white when the colours were at full strength.
  With the colours set to equal brightness on a light meter, the dogs still told them apart at one setting, but most struggled with red on green at another.
  At a third, one dog struggled with blue on yellow.
  The authors warn that brightness can throw colour tests done on screens.
  They also ran their colours through a popular "dog vision" photo filter, and found it got wrong what the dogs could actually see.

"Dogs see in black and white" is a myth.

## How sharp is a dog's vision?

**Fairly solid:** dogs see much less detail than people with normal sight.

- In Sweden, [8 dogs and 8 people](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0188557) were tested with cards of black and white stripes, made finer and finer until they could no longer tell them from plain grey.
  In good light, the dogs did about as well as a person with 20/30 to 20/110 vision; the people all did better than 20/20.
  In other words, a person can see the same detail from about three times as far away as a dog.
- A pilot test of [18 pet dogs](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsap.12965) watching stripes on a screen gave a typical value of about 20/80, ranging from about 20/40 to 20/250.
- Measuring the brain's response to stripes in [6 anaesthetised beagles](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.18-0334) gave about 20/40 or better.

The often-quoted "dogs see 20/75" comes from a [1995 review](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.1995.207.12.1623) that took a rough middle value from several older studies.
It is a fair ballpark, but dogs differ a lot from one another.

The dog eye is not the limit.
Dogs have a small spot packed with cones, [much like the human fovea](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0090390), and in theory it could support far sharper vision than dogs show in tests.
I found no study that explains the gap.

### Are some dogs short-sighted?

**Solid:** most dogs see about as well near as far, but some breeds are often short-sighted.

- Among [1,440 dogs of 90 breeds](https://doi.org/10.2460/ajvr.69.7.946) in the United States, the average dog was neither short- nor long-sighted.
  Toy Poodles were the most short-sighted breed, followed by Rottweilers, Collies and Miniature Schnauzers.
  Even in breeds that are usually fine, such as Labradors, about one dog in five was short-sighted.
  Short sight became more common with age.
- Among [99 mixed-breed dogs](https://doi.org/10.17221/106/2021-VETMED) in Poland, slight long-sightedness was more common than short sight.

How much a short-sighted dog is bothered by it is not known.

## Can dogs see in the dark?

**Partly.**
Dogs probably notice dim light better than we do, but they don't see detail better in the dark.

- Dogs' eyes have many more rods, the cells that work in dim light, than cones.
  They also have a reflective layer behind the retina, which is why their eyes shine in a torch beam.
  Both suggest good night vision, but I found no study that measured the dimmest light a dog can detect and compared it with people.
- In the Swedish study above, the lights were also turned down very low.
  The people [still saw about three times more detail](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0188557) than the dogs.
- In Vienna, [3 police dogs](https://doi.org/10.1167/4.3.10) were tested on telling apart shades of grey.
  They needed about twice as large a difference as people do in similar tests.

## Are dogs better at seeing movement?

**Not better than us, according to the only studies there are.**
All of them come from one lab in Padua, with five or six dogs each.

- Dogs and people watched clouds of moving dots on a screen, where some of the dots moved together and the rest moved at random.
  People could tell which way the dots were going when only 5% moved together; [dogs needed 42%](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-11864-z).
  With a lot of practice, the dogs got [down to 29%](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-018-1200-4), still far behind.
- In a test of [how slow a movement dogs can notice](https://doi.org/10.1093/cz/zoaa044), 6 dogs did better than earlier results for cats, rats and pigeons, but people do roughly ten times better.

Stories of police dogs spotting moving people from almost a kilometre away go back to a 1930s report that I could not trace to a scientific paper.

## How wide can dogs see?

The usual figure is about 240 degrees, with a smaller area in front seen by both eyes at once.
But the [1995 review](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.1995.207.12.1623) notes that this has hardly been measured, and the behavioural figure comes from two dogs in 1975.
I found no measurement of how field of view differs between long-nosed and flat-faced breeds.

You will often read that long-nosed dogs have a band of sharp vision across the horizon, while flat-faced dogs have a single sharp spot, like us.
That claim rests on a 2003 study of the retina that I could not read.

## How fast can dogs see: the frame-rate question

You will often read that dogs see TV as a flickering slideshow.
The idea comes from flicker: a light that turns on and off fast enough looks steady.

- The figure usually quoted is that dogs see flicker up to about 70 to 80 times a second, against about 60 for people.
  It comes from a 1989 study that I could not read.
- In 1995, that number led [a review](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.1995.207.12.1623) to suggest that a TV refreshing 60 times a second might look flickery to dogs.
  That was in the days of tube TVs, which really did flash their picture on and off.
- Modern flat screens [don't blank the picture between frames](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-017-1404-7) the way tube TVs did, so the flicker argument may no longer apply.

Frame rate and flicker are also not the same thing.
What matters for TV is whether dogs see smooth movement, and there the evidence is encouraging:

- In Vienna, [14 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-72506-5) watched a video, at 60 frames a second, of two people throwing a Frisbee.
  Eye-tracking showed that the dogs followed the Frisbee and looked ahead to the catcher before it arrived.

So "dogs see TV as a slideshow" is untested on modern screens, and what evidence there is suggests dogs follow video well.

## Do dogs watch TV?

**Many do, but what they make of it is unknown.**

- In Finland, dogs looking at photos on a monitor [looked at people and animals](https://doi.org/10.7717/peerj.10341) more than at the background, and at heads more than bodies.
- In a survey of [1,246 owners](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2023.106151), 86% said their dog watched screens at least sometimes; animals on screen got the most attention.
- A survey on [453 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-06580-y) found the same: animals on screen drove the reactions, along with the dog's temperament.

Whether dogs understand that the dog on TV stands for a real dog has not been tested.
The surveys rely on what owners report.

## What I take from this

- **Colour:** dogs see blues and yellows; reds and greens run together.
  For toys you want your dog to spot on grass, blue is the best bet.
- **Detail:** dogs see far less detail than we do, and individual dogs vary a lot.
- **Dim light:** dogs probably notice faint light better than us, but don't see detail better in the dark.
- **Movement:** dogs are not better than people at spotting movement in the studies we have.
- **TV:** the slideshow claim is untested on modern screens, and dogs follow video well.

Most of these findings come from small groups of dogs, often of one or two breeds, so expect your own dog to differ.
