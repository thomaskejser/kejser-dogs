# How long do dogs live?

Most pet dogs live about 12 years.
That is an average across all breeds; your own dog's breed and size, and the age it has already reached, change the answer a lot.

## What do the best studies say?

These are the largest independent studies of pet dogs of all breeds:

| Study                                                       | Where and what data                                                                     | Dogs                               | Headline figure                         |
| ----------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- |
| [O'Neill 2013](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tvjl.2013.09.020)  | England; records from first-opinion vets; deaths 2009–2011                              | 5,095 deaths                       | Median age at death **12.0 years**      |
| [McMillan 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-50458-w) | UK; 18 sources, including breed registries, insurers, vet groups and rehoming charities | 584,734 dogs, 284,734 of them dead | Median survival **12.5 years**          |
| [Lewis 2018](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-018-0066-8)     | UK; survey of owners of Kennel Club registered (pedigree) dogs; deaths 2004–2014        | 5,663 deaths                       | Median age at death **10.3 years**      |
| [Inoue 2018](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.17-0384)          | Tokyo; dogs buried or cremated at eight pet cemeteries, 2012–2015                       | 12,039 deaths                      | Life expectancy at birth **13.7 years** |

- **Life expectancy at birth** is the average (mean) age at death.
  A few deaths in young dogs pull the mean down, so the **median**, the age by which half the dogs have died, is usually a little higher.
  In the Tokyo data, the mean was 13.7 years and the median [14.0 years](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.17-0384).
- **Females live slightly longer**, by about [0.3 years](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-50458-w) in the largest UK study (median 12.7 against 12.4 years), but much of that gap is linked to neutering, which is a separate question.
- **I could not find an independent all-breed figure for the US.**
  The large US studies I found were funded by a pet-food company, which I don't cite, and the one independent US study gives a figure that is probably too high (see below).

## Why do the figures differ so much?

Published averages for dogs range from about [8.5 to 15.4 years](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.24.01.0068).
Most of that spread comes from how each study was done, not from real differences between dogs.

- **Where the data come from.**
  Vet records, insurance claims, pet cemeteries, breed registries and owner surveys each see a different slice of dogs.
  Studies also use [different measures](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.24.01.0068), such as mean age at death, median survival or a life table, which are not directly comparable.
- **Dogs still alive (right-censoring).**
  A study that counts only dogs that have died misses dogs born in the same years that are still alive, which [underestimates lifespan](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-020-00086-8).
  This is worst for breeds whose popularity is rising fast, because young dogs then [make up too much](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-50458-w) of the data.
  French Bulldogs, for example, came out at [9.8 years](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-50458-w) in the UK when living dogs were counted, and [10.2 years](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.17-0384) in Japan.
- **Dogs that join late (left truncation).**
  Vet and insurance records only see a dog once it is registered, so they cannot see dogs that died before.
  If dogs that join late are treated as if they had been followed from birth, the estimate comes out too high.
  The highest median in the literature, [15.4 years](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.24.01.0068), comes from a study of all dogs that happened to visit [three US clinics](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-020-00086-8) during one year.
  The authors do not discuss this, but every dog in that study had already survived long enough to be seen that year, and I think that is likely part of why the figure is so high.
- **Puppy deaths.**
  Many puppies die before they are ever registered with a vet.
  In Norwegian Kennel Club litters, [4.3% of puppies](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2011.12.023) were stillborn, 3.7% died in their first week and another 1% by 8 weeks of age (see [how many puppies are in a litter?](how-many-puppies-are-in-a-litter.md)).
  Whether a study counts from birth or from age 1 can change its figure, and an old study of Tokyo pet cemeteries found a lower life expectancy at birth (8.3 years) than at age 1 (8.6 years), [probably because](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.17-0384) owners at the time seldom buried young dogs there.
- **Euthanasia.**
  In a UK survey of owners of pedigree dogs, [80% of deaths](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-018-0066-8) involved euthanasia.
  So the age at death partly reflects owners' and vets' decisions about quality of life, and those decisions may differ between places and over time.

## How much do breeds differ?

A lot: breed medians span about 10 years.

- In the largest UK study, breed medians ranged from [5.4 years](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-50458-w) for the Caucasian Shepherd Dog to 15.4 years for the Lancashire Heeler.
  Mastiffs, Bulldogs, French Bulldogs and the Cane Corso were among the shortest-lived; small breeds such as the Tibetan Spaniel, Papillon and Border Terrier were among the longest-lived.
- In the same study, the median was [12.7 years](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-50458-w) for small breeds, 12.5 for medium and 11.9 for large breeds.

Single-breed figures are much less precise than all-breed ones, and the same breed can come out years apart in different studies.

## Are dogs living longer than they used to?

Probably, but the evidence is patchy, because no one has measured the same population in the same way over decades.

- **The overall trend.**
  A 2024 review tried to combine a dozen studies of dog lifespan from [1981 to 2023](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.24.01.0068).
  The studies were too different for a formal meta-analysis, but every study with data from 1997 or later reported a median of at least 10 years, and more recent studies tended to report longer lifespans.
  The authors conclude that dog lifespan has risen, not fallen as some newspapers have claimed.
- **Japan.**
  In dogs brought to Tokyo pet cemeteries, life expectancy rose from [8.6 years](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.17-0384) (counted from age 1) in 1981–1982 to 13.7 years (from birth) in 2012–2015.
  The 1980s data were biased, since young dogs were seldom buried there and owners rounded the ages of old dogs.
  In the early 1980s, dogs in Japan commonly died of heartworm, intestinal worms, distemper, traffic accidents and malnutrition; the authors credit better veterinary care, nutrition and welfare for the rise, but did not test this.

## How long will my dog live from here?

Longer than the average at birth suggests.
A dog that has reached a given age has already survived the risks of the years before, so its expected age at death rises as it gets older.

The only independent life table I found comes from the Tokyo pet cemeteries, and it gives the average number of years left at each age:

| Age now  | Years left | Average age at death |
| -------- | ---------- | -------------------- |
| Newborn  | 13.7       | 13.7                 |
| 2 years  | 12.0       | 14.0                 |
| 5 years  | 9.2        | 14.2                 |
| 8 years  | 6.4        | 14.4                 |
| 10 years | 4.8        | 14.8                 |
| 12 years | 3.5        | 15.5                 |
| 14 years | 2.3        | 16.3                 |
| 16 years | 1.6        | 17.6                 |

The figures come from [Inoue 2018](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.17-0384).

- **These are Tokyo dogs whose owners paid for burial or cremation.**
  Their median age at death, 14.0 years, is about 2 years higher than the UK medians above, so for a UK dog the ages in this table are probably too high.
  The pattern, not the exact numbers, is what carries over.
- **The risk of dying rises steeply after middle age.**
  In the Tokyo dogs, the chance of dying in any one year stayed [around 1% up to age 6](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.17-0384), passed 4% by age 9, and reached about 1 in 3 between ages 15 and 16.
- **The UK and US life tables I found were funded by a pet insurer or a pet-food company**, which I don't cite.

These are averages across many thousands of dogs.
They can help you plan, but they cannot predict how long one particular dog will live.

## What is still unknown

- **No country records dog deaths systematically.**
  Every figure above comes from a convenience sample: one vet network, one set of cemeteries, one breed registry or one group of insurers.
  The 2024 review calls for a [standard dog mortality record](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.24.01.0068) to fix this.
- **Long-term trends are uncertain**, because studies from different decades used different data and methods.
- **Lifespan is not the same as healthy lifespan.**
  These studies say how long dogs live, not how many of those years were spent in good health.
