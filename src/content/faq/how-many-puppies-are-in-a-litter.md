# How many puppies are in a litter?

A typical litter is five or six puppies, but the size of the breed matters more than anything else.
Toy breeds average two to four puppies; large breeds average six to eight.

Most puppies survive.
In the largest study I found, about 1 puppy in 12 was stillborn or died in its first week, and very few died after that.

## Typical litter size

The best data come from the Norwegian Kennel Club, where about 90% of pedigree dogs are registered.
Across [10,810 litters](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2010.10.034) of 224 breeds born in 2006 and 2007, the average was 5.4 puppies, counting stillborn ones.

| Breed size (adult weight) | Average litter |
| ------------------------- | -------------- |
| Miniature (under 5 kg)    | 3.5            |
| Small (5–10 kg)           | 4.2            |
| Medium (10–25 kg)         | 5.7            |
| Large (25–45 kg)          | 6.9            |
| Giant (over 45 kg)        | 7.1            |

Some breed averages from the same study:

- **Largest litters:** Rhodesian ridgeback [8.9](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2010.10.034), Leonberger and Dalmatian 8.4, golden retriever 7.5, Labrador retriever 6.9.
- **Smallest litters:** Chihuahua 3.2, miniature poodle 3.0, toy poodle and Pomeranian 2.4.

Litters within a breed vary much more than the averages suggest.
In the Norwegian data, [87% of the variation](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2010.10.034) in litter size was between litters of the same breed; Labrador litters, for example, ranged from 1 to 13 puppies.

Danish Kennel Club records of 115 breeds show the same pattern.
For breeds of middle size, each doubling of the breed's weight meant about [23% more puppies](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-026-00862-9), but the very smallest and very largest breeds had fewer puppies than that rule predicts.

Both studies rely on what breeders report.
Litters in which every puppy died were not registered, and stillborn puppies may be undercounted, so the true averages are probably slightly higher.

## What else affects litter size

- **The mother's age.**
  In Norway, litters [shrank steadily](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2010.10.034) with age in large and giant breeds; in small breeds, both very young and older mothers had smaller litters.
  For example, large-breed mothers averaged 7.2 puppies at three years and 6.0 at seven or older.
  Swedish Bernese mountain dogs showed the [same decline](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-022-00646-x).
- **Earlier litters (parity).**
  The evidence is mixed.
  In Norway, first litters looked largest, but that was explained by the mothers' younger age, and [litter number had no effect](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2010.10.034) once age was accounted for.
  In seven French breeds, litters grew until the [second or third litter](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tvjl.2014.11.008) and then shrank; among four-year-old Bernese mountain dogs, first litters were [smaller](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-022-00646-x) than later ones.
- **Inbreeding.**
  In seven French breeds, a litter from a full-brother and full-sister mating had about [0.65 fewer puppies](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tvjl.2014.11.008), and a mother who was herself that inbred had about 0.5 fewer.
  This was counted at registration, about two months after birth, so it mixes fewer puppies born with more puppies dying.
  In the Swedish Bernese mountain dogs, inbreeding had [no measurable effect](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-022-00646-x).
- **Flat faces.**
  Flat-faced breeds had litters about [25% smaller](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-026-00862-9) than expected for their weight; short legs made no difference.
  In Norway, pugs averaged [4.2 puppies](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2010.10.034) and French bulldogs 4.7, against 5.5 for beagles.
- **Artificial insemination.**
  After adjusting for breed and age, insemination with frozen semen meant about [1.3 fewer puppies](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2010.10.034) than natural mating.

I found no study showing that a caesarean itself changes how many puppies are born.
In the Danish records, [12.7% to 15.5%](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2025.117663) of all pedigree litters, and 37.6% to 40.1% of flat-faced litters, were born at least partly by caesarean.
In Bernese mountain dogs, litters of only one or two puppies were [more likely](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-022-00646-x) to need one.
See [flat-faced dogs](do-flat-faced-dogs-live-shorter-lives.md) for more on caesareans in those breeds.

[How many litters a female has over her life](how-many-litters-does-a-breeding-dog-have.md) is a separate question.

## How many puppies survive

In the Norwegian study of [58,439 puppies](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2011.12.023):

- 4.3% were stillborn;
- 3.7% were born alive but died in their first week;
- only about 1% died after the first week and before registration at about eight weeks;
- in all, 9.0% died before eight weeks, and 89% of those deaths happened at birth or in the first week.

The average litter therefore went from 5.4 puppies at birth to 4.97 at one week and 4.92 at eight weeks.
About [one litter in four](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2011.12.023) lost at least one puppy in the first week.

Other studies found more losses:

- **Four large breeds in Norway.**
  In 98 closely followed litters of Irish wolfhounds, Leonbergers, Labradors and Newfoundlands, [10.9%](https://doi.org/10.1186/1751-0147-49-S1-S2) of puppies were stillborn.
  Another 6.9% of the live-born puppies died within three weeks, most within three days, and only five more died before eight weeks.
- **Bernese mountain dogs in Sweden.**
  [15.4%](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-022-00646-x) of puppies were stillborn, and 19.1% died before registration.

The Norwegian authors could not rule out [under-reporting](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2011.12.023) of dead puppies, and suggest that close supervision is easier in Norway's small-scale home breeding.
They note that earlier, smaller studies from Australia and the UK found 18.5% and 26.3% of puppies stillborn or dead in the first week.

## Main causes of death

- **Difficult and long births.**
  In the four large breeds, litters with a stillborn puppy took [13.8 hours](https://doi.org/10.1186/1751-0147-49-S1-S2) to be born, against 7.0 hours for litters without one.
  In 42 Labrador litters, births longer than the median had [12.1% stillborn](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani16040600) puppies against 6.1% for shorter births, and puppies born from sixth place onward died more often.
  A stillbirth also signals trouble for the rest of the litter: litters with a stillborn puppy had [twice the odds](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2011.12.023) of losing another puppy in the first week.
- **Weak puppies, too little milk and crushing.**
  Among 46 puppies that died in the four large breeds, the breeders recorded [fading puppy syndrome](https://doi.org/10.1186/1751-0147-49-S1-S2) (an apparently normal puppy weakens and dies) in 9, very low birth weight in 7, crushing by the mother in 7, and starvation in 6 puppies of one litter whose mother had no milk.
  Puppies that died in the first week were lighter at birth than those that survived.
- **Birth defects and culling.**
  Some puppies with defects, or with colours that breed standards don't allow, are put to sleep.
  The Norwegian authors suspect this explains part of the [high first-week losses](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2011.12.023) in Dalmatians and Rhodesian ridgebacks, but the data cannot show it.

Infections are often named as a [major cause](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2011.12.023), but the studies of infection and of birth defects that I found either had funding statements I could not read or were funded by a vaccine or pet-food company, so I don't cite them.

## What affects survival

- **Litter size.**
  Bigger litters lose more puppies.
  In Norway, 3% to 4% of puppies were stillborn in litters of two to seven, against [11.7%](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2011.12.023) in litters of 12 or more; each extra puppy raised the odds of a stillbirth in the litter by 10%.
- **Breed size.**
  [5.9%](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2011.12.023) of puppies in miniature breeds died at birth or in the first week, against 11.6% in giant breeds.
  Individual breeds differed more than size groups: the Dogue de Bordeaux lost 24.6% of its puppies, the Dalmatian 19.5% and the pug 17.0%, while some breeds lost almost none.
  Many breeds had only a few dozen litters, so their figures are rough.
- **The mother's age and first litters.**
  Each extra year of the mother's age raised the odds that her litter lost a puppy around birth by [18%](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2011.12.023).
  First litters had the most losses, and a first litter after the age of six had three times the odds of a stillbirth.
- **Planned versus emergency caesarean.**
  At a US university hospital, [93.1%](https://doi.org/10.1111/vsu.13868) of 480 puppies delivered by caesarean survived to go home.
  Nearly all survived planned operations, against 80.4% of flat-faced and 88.7% of other puppies delivered in an emergency, after labour had gone wrong.
  Flat-faced breeds did no worse than others, but survival was only followed for the few hours until discharge.

All of these are associations in observational data.
For example, breeders may stop breeding from mothers that whelped badly, which can make later litters look safer than they are.

## Gaps in the evidence

- The large studies are of Scandinavian pedigree dogs; I found nothing comparable for mixed breeds or accidental litters.
- The largest data set, more than 200,000 French puppies, came from software owned by a pet-food company whose staff co-wrote the paper, so I don't use it.
- I found no large study I could cite that examined dead puppies to find the cause of death.
- Losses between one and eight weeks are reported only in Norway, and only as a total.
