# Do dogs know how tall they are?

Roughly, yes, in a practical sense.
Dogs judge whether they will fit through a gap, and they understand that their own body can be in the way.
But the sense is coarse, it seems to be learned, and almost all the evidence comes from one research group in Budapest.
Nobody has tested whether dogs know how high they can jump, or whether small dogs think they are big.

Some studies that would help are behind paywalls, and I could not read who funded them; they are listed on [papers I am looking for](../pages/papers-i-am-looking-for.md).

## Do dogs know whether they will fit through a gap?

Yes, roughly.
In several experiments, dogs walked through an opening in a wall to reach their owner or a treat, with the opening set to each dog's own size.

- **Dogs slow down at openings that are too small.** In the first study, from 2020, dogs [approached a too-small opening more slowly](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-019-01337-3) than a comfortable one, and an in-between opening at an in-between speed.
  When the opening shrank step by step, 19 of 42 dogs did not even try the one that was too small.
- **Short legs made no difference.** Short-legged breeds judged openings the same way as long-legged dogs.
- **Dogs take the long way round when a door is small.** Given a choice between a 7 m detour around a fence and a 2 m shortcut through a door, 68 family dogs [went around when the door was small](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-45241-w) and took a large door without hesitating.
  The same held for [57 mixed-breed dogs](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani15030432) and for [149 purebred dogs of 54 breeds](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani16010060), where breed made little difference.
- **An American study agreed.** In 43 dogs from very small to very large, the [smallest opening a dog managed went up with its height](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11030620), and the first thing nearly every dog did at a low opening was duck its head.
  This is the only study from outside the Budapest group.

### How precise is it?

Not very.
A Vienna group argued that a single opening can mislead, since a keen dog may simply rush through, and offered dogs two doors at once.
Dogs [picked a large door over a small one](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0338647) 75 to 97% of the time, but on their first try they chose at random between a large and a medium door.

Most of the results are about how fast dogs move, not whether they succeed.
The researchers themselves say it is unclear whether dogs carry a picture of their own body, or have learned from experience which gaps work.

## Do dogs know their body can get in the way?

Yes, it seems so.
In a test first used with toddlers and elephants, a dog stands on a small mat and is asked to hand its owner a toy.
In the test, the toy is tied to the mat, so the dog can only lift it by stepping off.

- In their first try, [27 of 32 dogs stepped off the mat](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-82309-x) when the toy was tied to it, against 14 of 32 when the toy was tied to the ground.
- Dogs also stepped off sooner when the toy was tied to the mat.
- The researchers checked that dogs were not simply stepping off because the mat felt odd.

## Does experience matter?

It seems to.

- **Agility dogs did better on shape.** In the Vienna study, dogs trained in agility [judged the shape of an opening](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0338647) better than untrained dogs, which suggests that practice sharpens the sense.
- **A new size takes learning.** In a small part of the American study, 13 dogs carrying a stick, which made them wider, [at first did not adjust](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11030620), but learned to with experience.
  The authors call this preliminary.

Every study tested adult dogs.
I found no study of how a growing puppy keeps up with its own size, or of old dogs.

## Do dogs know how high they can jump?

Nobody has tested it.
I found no study of dogs judging a jump onto a sofa or off a car.

The nearest is a study of reaching.
Nineteen dogs reached for a treat that was raised step by step, and the researchers noted the height at which each dog stopped stretching its neck and reared up on its hind legs.
Tall dogs [switched to rearing at a greater height](https://doi.org/10.3758/s13423-016-1183-6) than short dogs, but at the same height compared with their own shoulder.
So dogs judged what they could reach in proportion to their own size, as people do with their arm length.

## Do dogs judge the size of other dogs?

Yes, at least from a growl.

- **Matching a growl to a picture.** In a Vienna study of 96 dogs, a growl played between pictures of a small and a large dog; [20 of 24 dogs looked first at the dog whose size matched the growl](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0015175).
  Dogs shown triangles or cats instead, or played noise instead of a growl, showed no preference.
- **The same with life-size models.** In a British study, 40 dogs [looked longer at the model of a Jack Russell or a German shepherd](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0017069) that matched the growl.
- **Play growls sound bigger.** In a later Budapest study of 48 dogs, a food-guarding growl made dogs switch their gaze to the matching-size dog: 10 of the 11 that started on the wrong one did so.
  Dogs that heard a play growl instead [looked longer at the larger dog](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2013.07.013), whether or not it matched.
  The authors suggest dogs exaggerate their size in play, perhaps as part of signalling that it is a game.
- In the first two studies, the listening dog's own size made no difference.

So dogs can tell how big another dog is, but these studies say nothing about whether a dog compares that with its own size.

## Do small dogs think they are big?

Nobody has tested it.

- Small breeds do behave differently.
  In owner surveys on more than 8,000 dogs of 49 breeds, [shorter breeds had more reported problem behaviours](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0080529), including aggression towards their owners, fear of other dogs and begging.
- But that study shows that size and behaviour go together, not that small dogs misjudge their size.
  The authors suggest that the way owners treat small dogs could be part of the reason.
- In the gap studies, small dogs judged openings as well as large ones, which does not suggest they think they are bigger than they are.

There is one hint that small dogs try to seem bigger, which is not the same as thinking they are.

- In 45 adult male shelter dogs, [smaller males lifted their leg higher](https://doi.org/10.1111/jzo.12603) when they peed than larger males, though size explained only a small part of the difference.
- In 15 of the dogs, lifting the leg higher put the urine mark higher, so small males may leave marks higher for their size, as if to seem bigger to the next dog that sniffs them.
- The authors did not rule out simpler reasons: small dogs may aim higher to pee over other dogs' marks, and heavy dogs may keep their leg lower for balance.

## Do dogs recognise themselves?

Dogs fail the mirror test that apes pass, so they don't seem to recognise themselves by sight.
With smell it is less clear: dogs [sniffed their own urine longer when another smell had been added to it](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beproc.2017.08.001), which the author reads as a sign that they know their own smell.
Whether any mirror test, by sight or by smell, measures self-awareness is still argued about.

Knowing your own size does not need self-recognition; the researchers call it one part of how an animal represents its own body.

## What I take from this

- **Dogs have a working sense of their size.** It is enough to choose a large door over a small one, and to know their body is on the mat.
- **It is rough and learned.** Expect a dog to misjudge new situations, a cone collar or a gap of nearly the right size.
- **The rest is untested.** Jumping, puppies growing up, and small dogs "acting big" have not been studied.
