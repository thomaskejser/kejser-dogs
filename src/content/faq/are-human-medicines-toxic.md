# Are human medicines toxic to dogs?

Many human medicines are toxic to dogs.
Dogs are the species [most often poisoned by them](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tvjl.2014.11.004), usually after finding medicines that were not stored safely.

Depending on the medicine, poisoning can be severe.

## Signs of medicine poisoning

The signs depend on the medicine, so don't wait for them before calling your vet.

## How dangerous is it?

The medicines most often involved are:

- **Drugs acting on the brain**, such as antidepressants and sleeping pills
- **Painkillers** such as ibuprofen and other anti-inflammatory drugs
- **Heart and blood pressure medicines**, such as calcium channel blockers
- **Antihistamines**
- **Anti-diarrhoea medicine** (loperamide)
- **Supplements** containing vitamin D or iron
- **Asthma inhalers**

How dangerous a medicine is varies a lot:

- **Antidepressants** of the most common kind caused [no signs in 76% of 313 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1476-4431.2012.00805.x), and every dog that was followed up survived.
- **Anti-inflammatory painkillers**: [99% of 434 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1111/jvim.16603) survived with treatment, though ibuprofen caused worse signs than the dog medicine carprofen.
- **Baclofen**, a muscle relaxant, is more dangerous: [92% of poisoned dogs](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.241.8.1059) became ill, and about one in six died.

_Never_ give your dog a human medicine unless your vet tells you to.

### At what dose do symptoms appear?

This depends on the medicine.
For common painkillers, vets use these [toxic doses](https://doi.org/10.1111/jvim.16603):

| Medicine  | Stomach damage | Kidney damage  | Nervous system |
| --------- | -------------- | -------------- | -------------- |
| Ibuprofen | over 25 mg/kg  | over 100 mg/kg | over 400 mg/kg |
| Naproxen  | over 5 mg/kg   | over 25 mg/kg  | over 50 mg/kg  |

For example, a poodle of 3.8 kg could get stomach damage from about 95 mg of ibuprofen, which is less than half of a 200 mg tablet.

For baclofen, dogs that survived had eaten a median of 4.2 mg/kg, and dogs that died a median of 14 mg/kg.

## What to do?

Call your vet or an animal poison hotline straight away.
Note which medicine was eaten, its strength, roughly how much, and when.
Bring the packaging.

### What will the Vet do?

Treatment depends on the medicine, so the packaging helps your vet act quickly.
For painkiller poisoning, dogs that arrived later, or were not made to vomit, became more seriously ill.
Even large overdoses can be treated by cleaning the blood with plasma exchange: [61 of 62 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1111/jvim.16507) treated this way survived.
