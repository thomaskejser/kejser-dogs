Cancer, in most of the large studies.
After cancer come diseases of the bones and joints, brain and nerves, heart and urinary tract, mostly in older dogs.
Young dogs die of different things: accidents, infections, gut problems and behaviour problems.

Two things make the numbers less tidy than they look.
Most dogs are put to sleep, so the recorded "cause" is usually the reason for euthanasia.
And each study records causes in its own way, so the rankings differ between them.

## What the large studies found

| Study                                                                     | Dogs                                     | Most common causes of death                                            |
| ------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| [England, vet records](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tvjl.2013.09.020)        | 5,095 deaths among 102,609 dogs, 2009–11 | Cancer, musculoskeletal (bone and joint) disease, neurological disease |
| [UK, owner survey](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-018-0066-8)             | 5,663 deaths in pedigree dogs, 2004–14   | "Old age" 13.8%, unspecified cancer 8.7%, heart failure 4.9%           |
| [Japan, pet cemeteries](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.21-0171)             | 5,118 deaths, 2016–17                    | "Senility" 26%; cancer the leading disease                             |
| [US, teaching hospitals](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1939-1676.2011.0695.x) | 74,556 deaths, 1984–2004                 | Varied with age, size and breed (below)                                |

- **England.**
  In the first [VetCompass study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tvjl.2013.09.020), which used the records of 86 ordinary vet practices, the three leading groups were cancer, musculoskeletal and neurological disorders.
  A later paper by some of the same authors [puts them](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-018-0066-8) at 16.5%, 11.3% and 11.2% of deaths.
- **Japan.**
  In the [cemetery data](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.21-0171), owners most often named "senility", and among dogs that died of a disease, cancer came first.
- **Owner surveys** rank "old age" near the top, alongside cancer and heart disease; more on that below.
- **Other countries.**
  The large insurance and clinic studies I found for Sweden and Japan were funded by, or written by staff of, pet insurers or a pet-food company, which I don't cite.

## How the causes change with age

- **Puppies and young dogs.**
  In US [teaching hospitals](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1939-1676.2011.0695.x), young dogs died more often of gut and infectious diseases.
- **Accidents.**
  In a UK owner survey, death in a road accident had the [youngest median age](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-018-0066-8) of any cause, at about 3 years.
- **Behaviour problems** are a leading cause of death in young dogs; see the section on euthanasia below.
- **Older dogs.**
  Neurological disease and cancer [became more common](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1939-1676.2011.0695.x) with age, and so did deaths from heart, hormone (endocrine) and urinary disease.
  In [Japanese cemetery data](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.21-0171), the odds of death from cancer rose by 12% for each year of age.
- **The very old.**
  In the same Japanese data, the share of deaths from cancer peaked at 9 to 12 years; owners named "senility" more often than cancer for dogs that died after 15, and almost exclusively after 21.

## How the causes change with size and breed

- **Large dogs** die more often of cancer.
  In Japan, Golden Retrievers had [2.5 times the odds](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.21-0171) of dying of cancer, while small breeds such as the Chihuahua, Papillon, Toy Poodle, Yorkshire Terrier and Pomeranian had lower odds.
  In the US, larger breeds died more often of [musculoskeletal and gut disease](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1939-1676.2011.0695.x).
- **Small and toy dogs** more often die of other diseases.
  In the US, smaller breeds died more often of [endocrine disease](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1939-1676.2011.0695.x).
- **Breeds with a typical cause.**
  In a UK survey of Kennel Club breeds, [these stood out](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-018-0066-8):
  - Dobermanns: heart muscle disease (cardiomyopathy);
  - Cavalier King Charles Spaniels: cardiomyopathy and heart failure;
  - Bernese Mountain Dogs: lymphoma;
  - Flat-coated Retrievers, Rottweilers and Newfoundlands: bone cancer.
- **Flat-faced breeds.**
  The leading causes of death in Australian pugs were [breathing problems](https://doi.org/10.3390/vetsci12030195) from their short airways (8.2%), seizures (6.7%) and degenerative spinal cord disease (4.7%).

Neutering also shifts the pattern: in US teaching hospitals, neutered dogs [died less often](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0061082) of infections and more often of cancer than intact dogs.
That is an association in a hospital population, not proof that neutering causes cancer deaths.

## Most dogs are put to sleep

- In a UK survey of owners of pedigree dogs, [80% of deaths](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-018-0066-8) involved euthanasia, and the authors note that English vet records gave a similar figure, 86%.
- So "cause of death" is mostly the reason for euthanasia, recorded at the point where owner and vet [judged](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-018-0066-8) that the dog's life was no longer worth living.
- **Dogs that die on their own may die of different things**, but I could not find an independent study that compared them; the one I found was part-funded by a pet insurer, which I don't cite.
- **Behaviour problems end many young lives.**
  - Under three years of age, a third of deaths in English vet records ([33.7%](https://doi.org/10.7120/09627286.27.3.251)) were attributed to undesirable behaviour, more than the next cause, gut problems (14.5%).
    The commonest problem was aggression.
  - A study in Australia found [29.7%](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11020493) in dogs three years old or younger.
  - In another UK dataset, behaviour problems were the [leading cause of lost years of life](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-77385-8) in dogs from one to about seven years of age, and accounted for 31% of the lost years in dogs aged one to two.
  - In the UK owner survey, dogs that died of aggression did so at a [median age of about 3 years](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-018-0066-8).

## How good are these records?

All of these numbers come with weaknesses, and they explain much of the difference between studies.

- **Who records the cause.**
  - Owner surveys depend on memory and on which owners choose to answer.
  - Teaching hospitals see sicker dogs than ordinary practices, so the [US data](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1939-1676.2011.0695.x) over-represent serious and early illness.
- **"Old age" is not a diagnosis.**
  - Owners report it often: [14%](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-018-0066-8) of deaths in a UK survey, which counted another 4% as old age combined with signs such as arthritis or incontinence.
  - In [Japanese cemetery data](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.21-0171), "senility" was the commonest cause, at 26%, but owners were given no definition of it.
    The authors suggest owners rarely take a dog dying of old age to the vet, which is why "senility" is rare in vet and insurance data.
  - "Old age" can hide undiagnosed disease: in the same data, dogs that lived indoors were [less likely](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.21-0171) to have senility recorded, and the authors suggest their owners were more likely to notice illness.
- **Few post-mortems.**
  Post-mortems are rare: [under 6%](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-018-0066-8) of deaths in one UK survey.
- **How causes are grouped.**
  Studies group causes either by the [organ affected or by the kind of disease](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1939-1676.2011.0695.x), such as cancer or infection.
  A lymphoma, for example, can count as cancer in one system and as a blood disease in the other, so rankings from different studies are not directly comparable.
  Newer studies use [language models](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-77385-8) to code free-text records, which handles far more records but misclassifies some of them.
- **Which dogs are studied.**
  Breed mix differs between countries and between data sources, and breed shapes what dogs die of.

No study has followed a representative group of dogs from birth to death with a confirmed diagnosis for every death.
The best evidence comes from large vet-record studies, and they agree that cancer is the leading recorded cause in older dogs.
