Yes, on average.
In every large lifespan study I found, flat-faced breeds died younger than breeds with a medium-length muzzle: by about a year and a half in the UK, and the bulldogs by about three years.

Lifespan is only part of the story.
Flat-faced dogs also live with much more illness: blocked airways, overheating, eye ulcers, skin-fold infections and difficult births.
For breathing and eye problems, the risk rises the flatter the face, and dogs with longer muzzles are much less affected.

## What "flat-faced" means

Vets call a shortened face **brachycephaly**.
Pugs, French bulldogs, bulldogs, Pekingese, shih tzus, Boston terriers and boxers are common examples, although studies don't all count the same breeds.

It is a matter of degree, not a yes-or-no trait.
Researchers measure it as the **craniofacial ratio**: the length of the muzzle divided by the length of the skull behind it.
In one study, an extreme pug had a ratio of [0.08](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0137496), a bulldog cross 0.23 and a boxer 0.35.

## The lifespan evidence

- **UK, 584,734 dogs.**
  In the largest study, flat-faced purebreds had a median lifespan of [11.2 years](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-50458-w), against 12.8 for breeds with a medium-length muzzle, and a 40% higher risk of death at any age.
  - The French bulldog and the bulldog both had a median of [9.8 years](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-50458-w), and the pug 11.6.
  - Flat faces cost most in bigger dogs: large flat-faced breeds had a median of [10.7 years](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-50458-w), against 13.3 for small long-faced breeds.
- **UK vet records, 28,159 dead dogs.**
  Flat-faced breeds died at an average of [10.95 years](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-77385-8), against 12.51 for breeds with a medium-length muzzle.
  They also had 1.19 times the odds of a premature death, which the study defined against each breed's own typical lifespan.
  Causes of death were read from vet notes by a language model, so some are misclassified.
- **Portugal, 278,116 deaths in the national registry.**
  Flat-faced breeds had a mean life expectancy at birth of [8.0 years](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani14152141), against 9.5 for breeds with a medium-length muzzle.
  The French bulldog had the lowest of all 79 breeds, at about 6.3 years, and skull shape had a larger effect on lifespan than body size.
- **Australia, 691 pugs.**
  Pugs seen by ordinary vets died at a median age of [10 years](https://doi.org/10.3390/vetsci12030195).
  The leading causes were breathing problems from the short airway, seizures and spinal cord disease.
  The commonest reason for putting a pug to sleep was neurological disease; among pugs that died on their own, breathing problems were the commonest cause.

Bulldogs and pugs die of more than their faces; see [what dogs usually die of](/faq/what-do-dogs-usually-die-of) for how causes of death differ by breed.

### Association, not proof

All of these studies compare breeds, so they show an association.
Several things make the gap hard to measure exactly:

- **Young populations.**
  The French bulldog became popular quickly; in Portugal, registrations rose from [1,234 in 2013 to 7,554 in 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani14152141).
  When most dogs of a breed are young, the ones that have died so far died young, which pulls its estimate down.
- **Missing dogs.**
  Pet insurers are a common data source, and they [under-represent](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-50458-w) breeds with high disease risk, such as flat-faced breeds, because their premiums are high.
- **Breed is a bundle.**
  Breeds differ in much more than their faces, and a breed comparison can't separate the face from everything else.

The evidence that the face itself matters comes from the health studies below, where disease risk rises as the muzzle shortens, even within one breed.
No study I found followed dogs with different muzzle lengths within a breed to see whether the longer-muzzled ones live longer.

## Breathing problems

The main problem is **brachycephalic obstructive airway syndrome (BOAS)**.
Breeding shortened the bones of the face, but [not the soft tissue](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0137496) inside it, so the soft palate, tongue and nasal tissue crowd the airway.
Affected dogs snore, struggle with exercise and heat, sleep badly, and in the worst cases [collapse](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0181928).

How common it is:

- **Pugs, French bulldogs and bulldogs.**
  Among 604 dogs examined in a Cambridge study, [64.6%](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0181928) of pugs, 58.9% of French bulldogs and 51.2% of bulldogs had BOAS severe enough to need veterinary treatment.
  The dogs came through breed clubs, breeders and volunteer owners, so they are not a random sample.
- **Dogs put forward for breeding in Finland.**
  Among dogs taking the Finnish Kennel Club's breathing test, [28%](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-024-00755-9) of bulldogs, 22% of French bulldogs and 30% of pugs had moderate to severe signs.
  These are dogs their owners wanted to breed from, so they are probably healthier than average.
- **Other flat-faced breeds.**
  In 898 dogs of [14 further breeds](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0340604), almost every breed had some dogs with abnormal breathing.
  The Pekingese and Japanese chin were worst: only 10.9% and 17.4% of them breathed normally.
  Boston terriers did better than the bulldogs: [37.5%](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0315411) breathed normally, against 10% of French bulldogs and 15.2% of bulldogs.

Owners often see these signs as normal for the breed.
In a survey of 2,168 owners of pugs, French bulldogs and bulldogs, [17.9%](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0219918) reported breathing problems, 36.5% problems coping with heat and 19.9% at least one operation for the dog's shape.
Yet 70.9% rated their dog's health as very good or the best possible.

## Overheating

Dogs cool down by panting, and a blocked airway makes that harder.

- In the records of 905,543 UK dogs, flat-faced dogs had [2.1 times the odds](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-66015-8) of heat-related illness of dogs with a medium-length muzzle.
  In one year, 0.42% of bulldogs were treated for it, against 0.04% of all dogs, and 14% of all cases were fatal.
- Once a dog had heat-related illness, a flat face meant [3 times the odds](https://doi.org/10.3390/vetsci9050231) of dying of it.

## Eyes and skin

- **Corneal ulcers.**
  In 700 dogs at a UK referral hospital, flat-faced dogs were [20 times as likely](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0123827) to have had a corneal ulcer, a painful wound on the surface of the eye that can cause blindness.
  A fold of skin over the nose (nasal fold) made it nearly five times as likely, and a wider eye opening raised the risk further.
  Only 31 dogs had ulcers, so these multiples are rough.
- **Skin folds.**
  In a study of pugs and pug crosses, [38.3%](https://doi.org/10.1017/awf.2026.10095) of pugs had an inflamed nasal fold, against 2% of Jack Russell crosses and none of the beagle crosses.
  One in five pugs had entropion, an eyelid that rolls inward so the lashes rub the eye.

I did not find an independent study of dental disease in flat-faced breeds whose funding I could check.
The large UK vet-record studies of eye, skin and dental disorders in these breeds were part-funded by a pet insurer, which I don't cite.

## Difficult births

Many flat-faced litters are born by caesarean section.
In the Danish Kennel Club's records of [35,121 litters](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2025.117663) from 2013 to 2022:

- 37.6% to 40.1% of litters in flat-faced breeds were born at least partly by caesarean, against 12.7% to 15.5% across all pedigree breeds;
- in English bulldogs, 67% of litters were born entirely by caesarean and another 14% partly;
- in French bulldogs, the figures were 44% and 18%.

Some of these operations are planned in advance, so the numbers reflect breeders' choices as well as how many bitches can't give birth naturally.

## Does surgery help?

Surgery [widens the airway](https://doi.org/10.1111/vsu.12608), for example by opening the nostrils and shortening the soft palate.
It helps, but it does not make the airway normal.

- In 50 pugs, French bulldogs and bulldogs, a measure of breathing obstruction fell from a median of [76% to 63%](https://doi.org/10.1111/vsu.12608) one to six months after surgery, which was still in the affected range.
  Dogs with a collapsing larynx did worse.
- In a follow-up about four and a half years after surgery, the improvement had [lasted](https://doi.org/10.1111/vsu.70034).
  Only 32 of 117 dogs could be reassessed, and 39% of their owners still thought their dog had breathing problems.

I found no study of whether surgery lengthens life.

## Do longer muzzles and crossbreeding help?

For breathing and eyes, yes; for lifespan, nobody has measured it yet.

- **Muzzle length.**
  In a UK study of 854 dogs, BOAS occurred [only in dogs](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0137496) whose muzzle was less than half the length of the skull behind it, and the risk rose steeply as the muzzle got shorter.
  A pug with the shortest muzzle in the study had a predicted risk of 95%; one with the longest, 48%.
- **Nostrils and weight.**
  Narrow nostrils [raised the risk](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0181928) in all three of the most affected breeds, and being overweight in pugs and bulldogs.
  Across 14 other breeds, a shorter face, narrow nostrils and extra weight together explained only [20% of the variation](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0340604), and some very flat-faced breeds, such as the King Charles spaniel, had less BOAS than expected, so each breed needs its own approach.
- **Breeding tests.**
  In Finland, BOAS grade was [moderately to highly heritable](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-024-00755-9), so breeding from less affected dogs should produce less affected puppies.
  By 2022, 89% to 100% of registered bulldog, French bulldog and pug litters had at least one tested parent, but whether such schemes improve a breed's health is [not yet known](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-024-00755-9).
- **Crossbreeding.**
  In a UK study of 51 pugs and 99 pug crosses with a Jack Russell terrier or a beagle, the crosses had muzzles more than [twice as long](https://doi.org/10.1017/awf.2026.10095) relative to the skull.
  None of the pugs breathed normally, against all but one of the crosses, and the pugs also had more eye, skin and joint problems and cost their owners about four times as much at the vet.
  The dogs were volunteered by their owners, and the study did not follow them to death.

## What this means for you

If you are choosing a puppy and want the best odds of a long, healthy life, a breed with a normal-length muzzle is the safest choice.
If you want a flat-faced type, a dog with a longer muzzle, open nostrils and parents with a good breathing grade lowers the risk, though nobody has shown it lengthens life.

If you already have a flat-faced dog:

- keep it slim, because extra weight makes breathing worse (see [keeping your dog slim](/faq/will-keeping-my-dog-slim-help-it-live-longer));
- keep it cool and avoid hard exercise on warm days;
- treat snoring, noisy breathing and tiring easily as signs of disease, not of the breed, and ask your vet whether surgery would help;
- take squinting or a cloudy or weeping eye to the vet promptly.
