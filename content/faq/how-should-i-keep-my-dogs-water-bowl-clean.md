Wash your dog's water bowl often, with hot water and soap or in a dishwasher.
No study has tested how often a water bowl needs washing, so the advice comes from studies of food bowls and of what grows in drinking water.

## What grows in pet bowls

- **Hand washing did no better than wiping.** Bowls that were only wiped dry carried as many bacteria as bowls [washed by hand](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-023-03823-w) in an Italian study of 96 dog food bowls.
- **Drug-resistant bacteria turn up in water bowls.** _E. coli_ grew in [44% of water samples](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-024-04435-8) taken from pet cats' bowls in Thailand, and some carried genes for resistance to common antibiotics.
- **Bowls can harbour bacteria that cause ear infections.** In dogs with ear infections caused by _Pseudomonas_, the [same strain](https://doi.org/10.1111/vde.12347) was found in seven of the dogs' water bowls, which suggests a waterborne source for some dogs.
- **_Pseudomonas_ forms biofilms**: slimy layers that let it [survive on moist surfaces](https://doi.org/10.3390/antibiotics14070720) and resist disinfectants.

No study has looked for _Salmonella_, MRSA or _Legionella_ in dogs' water bowls; the Italian study found no _Salmonella_ in the food bowls it tested.

## How to clean the bowl

- **Use hot water.** In a US trial, washing food bowls with hot water above 71 °C or in a dishwasher [removed more bacteria](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0259478) than washing with cold or lukewarm water.
- **A dishwasher helps.** Bowls washed by hand tended to carry more gut bacteria than bowls from a dishwasher, though only five bowls in the [Italian study](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-023-03823-w) were washed by machine.
- **Scrub the inside** to break up the biofilm, then rinse and refill with fresh water.
- **Wash it every day or two.** This is a reasonable routine rather than a tested one: the US trial asked owners to wash food bowls after each use, and few kept it up.

### Which material is best?

The evidence does not settle this.
Stainless steel is usually recommended, but metal food bowls carried [more bacteria than plastic ones](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-023-03823-w) in the Italian study, while the US study found no difference by material.
The Italian authors note that the grade, smoothness and age of the steel affect how easily it cleans, so a smooth, unscratched bowl is the safer choice.

## Water fountains

Fountains are sold as cleaner and as a way to make pets drink more.
Neither claim has been tested in dogs.

- **No more drinking, at least in cats.** Laboratory cats drank [the same amount](https://doi.org/10.1177/1098612X18803753) from still, circulating and free-falling water bowls, and pet cats drank only [slightly more](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jfms.2009.10.008) from a fountain than a bowl, without more dilute urine.
- **No evidence they are cleaner.** No study has measured bacteria in pet fountains.
  A pump, tubing and filter are more moist surfaces for biofilm, so a fountain needs taking apart and cleaning as often as a bowl.

## Other ideas about how dogs should drink

| Dogs prefer moving water                 | Not studied in dogs. Cats did not drink more from moving water.                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                |
| Dogs prefer fresh water                  | Not studied directly. Dogs did drink more from a bowl with a [scent they liked](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2025.1688084), so what a bowl smells like can matter to them.                                                                                                                                                                    |
| Raised bowls prevent bloat               | The evidence points the other way. In 1,637 large and giant dogs, a raised food bowl was linked to [more cases of bloat](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.2000.217.1492) (gastric dilatation-volvulus), and a [review](https://doi.org/10.18849/ve.v2i1.57) found no study where raising the bowl lowered the risk. Water bowls were not studied. |
| Ice or cold water is dangerous           | I found no study showing harm. After hard exercise, drinking ice water was the only method in one test that [lowered dogs' core temperature](https://doi.org/10.2460/ajvr.25.06.0228).                                                                                                                                                         |
| Shared bowls at dog parks spread disease | Not studied directly. Dogs that visit dog parks [more often carry _Giardia_](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.vetpar.2011.08.019) and [get _Pseudomonas_ ear infections](https://doi.org/10.1111/vde.12347), but no study has traced this to shared bowls. Bringing your own bowl is a cheap precaution.                                              |

## How much water does a dog need?

It varies too much for a fixed figure, so always leave clean water out.

- **Diet matters.** Dogs fed dry food [drink more](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2025.1688084) than dogs fed wetter food.
- **Work matters.** Working dogs' daily water needs [depend mainly on how hard they work](https://doi.org/10.1111/jvim.15091).
- **Too much can be a sign of illness.** Drinking more than about [90 to 100 ml per kg](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsap.70105) of body weight a day is treated as excessive, though healthy dogs vary widely.
  If your dog starts drinking much more than usual, see a vet.
