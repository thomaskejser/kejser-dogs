Chocolate is toxic to dogs.
It contains **theobromine** and [caffeine](/faq/is-caffeine-toxic), which dogs break down far more slowly than people do.

High doses can result in coma and death.

## How dangerous is it?

The risk depends on the type of chocolate and the size of the dog:

- **Cocoa powder and baking chocolate** are the most dangerous.
- **Dark chocolate** is dangerous even in small amounts.
- **Milk chocolate** is less concentrated, but still risky for small dogs.

Exposure is toxic to all breeds

## Signs of chocolate poisoning

- Vomiting and diarrhoea
- Restlessness, panting and a racing heart
- Tremors or seizures in severe cases: death

### At what dose do symptoms appear?

Symptoms appear with a theobromine dose of only [20 mg/kg](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2016.00026).
This is a very small amount of dark chocolate for a dog to eat!

For example, my poodle of 3.8kg would only need to eat a single piece of dark chocolate
the size of my fingertip to experience symptoms.

### What dose is fatal?

A [laboratory study](https://doi.org/10.1016/0041-008X%2880%2990360-9) done in 1980 gave dogs measured doses of pure theobromine.
Of 33 dogs given a single dose between 15 and 1,000 mg/kg, three died: one each at 300, 500 and 1,000 mg/kg.

This study would be unlikely to pass ethical review today.
But I am citing it here because it established the rough, fatal dose.

## What to do?

Call your vet or an animal poison hotline straight away.
Note what kind of chocolate was eaten, roughly how much, and when.

If your dog gets to the vet - it has a good chance of full recovery (citation needed!)

### What will the Vet do?

Vets will observe your dog and make sure enough fluid is administered.
This will typically involved an IV-drip.
Expect that the vet might shave a bit of hair off one leg for the drip to enter.

The vet may also make the dog vomit with the drug apomorphine.

Activated charcoal is also used to limit the effects of the already ingested chocolate.
