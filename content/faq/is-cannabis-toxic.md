Cannabis is toxic to dogs.
Dogs can be poisoned by eating the plant, joints, or food made with cannabis.

Most dogs recover fully, but deaths have been reported, including two dogs that ate baked goods made with medical-grade THC butter.

Eating cannabis is best studied, but passively inhaling smoke is known to have an effect too.

## Signs of cannabis poisoning

Signs start within minutes of breathing in smoke, or within [several hours](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0261909) of eating cannabis, and usually pass within 72 hours.
In [223 poisoned dogs](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.24.02.0092), the most common signs were:

- Wobbling (88%)
- Over-sensitivity to touch and sound (75%)
- Lethargy (63%)
- Leaking urine (46%)
- Vomiting (26%)

A slow heartbeat is also common.

## How dangerous is it?

- **Most dogs recover**: all 223 dogs in the largest study survived.
- **Deaths are rare but reported**: vets in a survey reported 16 deaths, from causes such as pneumonia from inhaled vomit, breathing failure and seizures, though other causes could not always be ruled out.
- **Edibles** were the most common suspected cause in a survey of vets.
- **Dogs find it outdoors too**: in Melbourne, [15 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1111/avj.13134) were poisoned after eating human faeces, mostly in parks.
- **Cases rise with legalisation**: in Colorado, cases at two veterinary hospitals rose fourfold over five years, in step with medical cannabis licences, and US poison centre calls about cannabis were [more common in states with lighter penalties](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0250323).

### At what dose do symptoms appear?

No lethal dose has been found: laboratory dogs survived oral doses of up to 3,000 mg/kg of THC, the main active compound.
The strength of cannabis products varies widely, and the amount of THC in a dog's blood [did not predict](https://doi.org/10.1111/vec.13428) how ill it was.

## What to do?

Call your vet or an animal poison hotline straight away.
Note what was eaten, roughly how much, and when.

### Be honest with your vet

Vets usually cannot prove cannabis poisoning with a quick test, so what you tell them matters.

- **Human drug tests often miss it**: dogs break THC down differently from people, and the urine screens look for a breakdown product that dogs make little of.
  In [19 poisoned dogs](https://doi.org/10.1177/10406387211027227), the screen was negative for every dog it was run on, although THC was found in every dog's blood.
  An [earlier study](https://doi.org/10.5326/0450059) found a human test kit worked for several other drugs but not for cannabis in dogs.
  A [small 2025 study](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2025.1679400) found one test positive in all nine dogs known to have eaten cannabis, but without lab confirmation.
- **Lab confirmation is slow and rare**: mass spectrometry can confirm it, but usually [takes several days](https://doi.org/10.1053/j.tcam.2013.03.004), and in the 2025 study no lab in the region offered it.
- **The diagnosis rests on your story and the dog's signs**: that is how [most vets in a survey](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0261909) diagnosed it.
- **Without your story, the vet must rule out other causes**: other medicines and drugs, some parasite treatments, xylitol and hallucinogenic mushrooms can cause similar signs, and checking for them takes extra tests, time and money.
- **Mention edibles**: brownies, chocolates and sweets may also contain chocolate or xylitol, which are poisonous on their own.

In the largest study, 68% of owners denied that their dog could have eaten cannabis, and the authors advise vets to suspect it anyway when the signs fit.

### What will the Vet do?

Most dogs are monitored until the effects wear off, often at home, and most recover completely.

## Will my vet report me?

Generally not: vets must keep what you tell them confidential, and we found no law that requires them to report cannabis use or a pet's exposure.
Suspected animal abuse is the exception.
This is general information, not legal advice, and rules differ between countries and states.

- **Vets keep client information confidential**: the [AVMA ethics principles](https://www.avma.org/resources-tools/avma-policies/principles-veterinary-medical-ethics-avma) in the US, the [FVE European code](https://www.fve.org/cms/wp-content/uploads/FVE_Code_of_Conduct_2019_R1_WEB.pdf) and the [RCVS code](https://www.rcvs.org.uk/setting-standards/advice-and-guidance/code-of-professional-conduct-for-veterinary-surgeons/supporting-guidance/client-confidentiality/) in the UK allow disclosure only with consent, when the law requires it, or to protect people or animals.
- **Some laws back this up**: in California, a vet [may not disclose](https://leginfo.legislature.ca.gov/faces/codes_displaySection.xhtml?lawCode=BPC&sectionNum=4857) information about a client or patient except in listed cases, such as with consent or under a court order or subpoena.
  In Germany, a vet who reveals a client's secrets [commits a crime](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/stgb/__203.html).
  In the EU and UK, data protection law ([GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)) also protects client data.
- **No duty to report drug use was found**: we found no US or European law that requires vets to report a client's cannabis use or a pet's exposure, though we could not check every state and country.
- **Abuse is a separate matter**: about [24 US states](https://www.animallaw.info/topic/table-veterinary-reporting-requirement-and-immunity-laws) require vets to report suspected cruelty or neglect, and the RCVS lets UK vets break confidentiality when an animal is at real risk of harm.
  These duties concern deliberate harm or neglect, not an accident.
- **A few rules go further**: New Mexico's veterinary rules say that reporting ["cruelty or illegal action"](https://www.animallaw.info/administrative/nm-veterinary-reporting-162538-conduct) is not a breach of confidentiality, so a vet there may report, though they are not required to.
- **Cannabis law varies**:
  - **US**: federal law still controls cannabis; only FDA-approved and state-licensed medical cannabis [moved to Schedule III](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/04/28/2026-08176/schedules-of-controlled-substances-rescheduling-of-food-and-drug-administration-approved-products) in 2026, and state laws differ widely.
  - **Germany**: since April 2024, adults may [possess](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/kcang/__3.html) up to 25 g in public, and up to 50 g and three plants at home.
  - **Netherlands**: cannabis is illegal, but possessing up to 5 g is [tolerated](https://www.government.nl/topics/drugs/toleration-policy-regarding-soft-drugs-and-coffee-shops).
  - **UK**: cannabis is a [Class B drug](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1971/38/schedule/2).
