Every dog owner has heard a story of a dog that went wild with joy when someone came home after a year abroad.
I wanted to know whether science backs those stories up with a number.
It doesn't.

What experiments do show is that dogs know the people they live with, and can pick them out by face, by voice and by smell.
What no study I found has done is take dogs away from a person for months or years and then test whether they still recognise them.

## Dogs recognise their people by face

**Solid:** dogs can tell their owner's face from other faces, at least close up and in good conditions.

- In Vienna, [15 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.lmot.2013.04.005) were trained to pick one of two people who put their heads through holes in a screen: their owner, or another person they had known for years.
  All but one dog learned to pick the right person.
  When the real heads were replaced by life-sized photos, 10 of 14 dogs still managed it, but when only the eyes, nose and mouth were shown, just 2 of 10 did.
  So dogs seem to rely on the whole head, including hair and outline, more than on the inner face.
- In Padua, [60 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-020-01382-3) were briefly separated from their owners and could only rejoin them by going to the spot marked with a photo of the owner's face rather than a stranger's.
  With well-lit, front-facing photos, they chose the owner's photo more often than chance.
- In Kyoto, pet dogs heard either their owner or a stranger call their name, then saw a photo of one of the two faces.
  The dogs [looked longer when the face did not match the voice](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-006-0025-8), which suggests that hearing the owner's voice brought the owner's face to mind.

## Dogs recognise their people by voice

**Fairly solid**, from one well-controlled study.

In Budapest, [28 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-022-01601-z) had to find their owner, who was hiding behind one of two screens, with a stranger behind the other.
Loudspeakers in front of each screen played recorded sentences read from recipes, so the words carried no meaning for the dog.

- The dogs went to the owner's voice in 82% of trials.
- When the researchers swapped the voices, so that the owner's voice came from the screen hiding the stranger, the dogs still went to the owner's voice.
  They were following the voice, not the owner's smell.
- Dogs hesitated longer when the two voices were similar in pitch.

Of the 43 dogs that started, 14 were left out because they did not pass the training stage, so this tells us what many dogs can do, not every dog.

## Dogs recognise their people by smell

**Suggestive:** two small studies point the same way.

- In Germany, [54 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-82952-4) followed a scent trail laid 15 minutes earlier by one of two people they knew well, usually their owners.
  When the person at the end of the trail was the other one, the dogs became more active than when it was the person whose trail they had followed.
  They seemed to expect a particular person, not just "a familiar smell".
  But 16 of the 54 dogs did not go to the person at all in the first three minutes, so dogs differed a lot.
- In Atlanta, [12 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beproc.2014.02.011) trained to lie still in a brain scanner smelled five scents: their own, a familiar person, a stranger, a familiar dog and a strange dog.
  The familiar person was someone from the household or a close friend, and was not in the room.
  A brain area linked to expecting something good, the caudate, responded most to the familiar person.
  This shows that the dogs told the scents apart and liked that one, but brain scans of 12 dogs say little about how long the memory lasts.

## Dogs react more strongly to their own people

In Japan, dogs' tear volume was measured with a small paper strip.
In [18 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2022.07.031), it rose when their owner came back after 5 to 7 hours away.
In a second test at day-care centres, dogs produced more tears when reunited with their owner than with a familiar person who was not their owner.

This is one lab and a new measure, so I treat it as a hint rather than a fact.
How the length of an absence of a few hours changes a dog's greeting is covered in [Do dogs have a sense of time?](/faq/do-dogs-have-a-sense-of-time).

## People a dog has met only once

The one study I found suggests that a single meeting is not enough.
Dogs that had been helped by a stranger once [did not pick that person out](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-022-01624-6) a week later, although they seemed to remember that someone had helped them.
I describe that study in [Do dogs remember past events?](/faq/do-dogs-remember-past-events).

So recognition seems to need time together, and the studies above all tested people the dogs lived with or saw often.

## What about months or years apart?

This is the question owners really care about, and it is the one I could not answer.

I found no study I can cite that separated dogs from a person they knew well for months or years and then tested whether they recognised that person.
All the studies above tested people the dogs had seen that day or recently.

Two older studies come closer, but I leave them out:

- A short 1999 conference chapter tested dogs with the scent of the people who had cared for them as puppies.
  I could not read the full text or check who paid for it.
- A 1994 study of dogs and their mothers after two years apart is often quoted.
  It is about dogs recognising dogs, not people, and I could not read it either.

How long dogs remember other things is a separate question.
In one study, a few unusually gifted dogs still knew some toy names two years later, which is covered in [How long can a dog remember things?](/faq/how-long-is-a-dogs-memory).
That shows a dog's memory _can_ last years, but toy names are not people, and those dogs were rare.

## Owners' stories

Videos of dogs greeting soldiers home from deployment are moving, and I have no reason to doubt them.
But they are not evidence of how long dogs remember, for several reasons:

- **We only see the happy ones.** Nobody shares a video of a dog that ignores its returning owner.
- **The person gives it away.** A returning owner calls the dog by name, in a familiar voice, often in the dog's own home, and is wildly excited.
  As the voice study shows, the voice alone can be enough for recognition.
- **There is no comparison.** Many dogs greet any friendly visitor eagerly, and no video shows how the same dog greets an excited stranger.

None of this means the stories are wrong.
It means they cannot tell us whether the dog recognised the person, or for how long it would have.

## What I take from this

- **Recognition:** dogs know the people they live with by face, voice and smell.
- **Strength of the bond:** dogs react more to their own people than to other familiar people.
- **Duration:** unknown.
  The longest gaps tested in the studies I could use were hours, not months.

If you are going away for a long time, I can't promise from the evidence that your dog will remember you.
I also found nothing to suggest it will forget you.
