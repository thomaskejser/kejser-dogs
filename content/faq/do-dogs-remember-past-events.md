"Dogs live in the moment" is a common saying, and a comforting one.
The experiments I found don't support it.
Dogs remember single events from their past, including things they had no reason to think they would need to remember.

What the experiments cannot show is what that memory feels like to the dog.
That is why researchers call it **episodic-like** memory rather than episodic memory.

## What "episodic-like" means

In people, **episodic memory** is memory of specific events in your own life: what happened, where, and when.
It is usually thought to involve a sense of yourself in the past, of "being there again".
Whether any other animal has this is [a debated question](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2016.09.057), because there is no way to ask an animal what it experiences when it remembers.

So researchers test what they can observe, using one of two definitions:

- **What, where and when.** The animal remembers the content of an event, its place and its timing.
  This approach started with experiments on food-hoarding scrub jays, as the [authors of one dog study](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-67302-0) describe.
- **Incidental encoding.** The animal remembers something it did not know it would need later, shown by a memory test it could not have expected.
  This rules out the animal having simply learned a rule such as "watch carefully, there will be a test".

Passing either test makes a memory "episodic-like".
It does not show that the dog consciously relives the event; nobody can test that, in dogs or in any other animal.

## The evidence

### Remembering what someone else did

In a study from Budapest, [17 pet dogs](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2016.09.057) had already been trained to copy their owner's actions on the command "Do it!".
The researchers then retrained them so that, after watching the owner do something, the dogs expected to be told to lie down instead.
Then, without warning, the owner said "Do it!" again.

- At baseline, when the dogs expected to copy, 16 of 17 did the action they had seen.
- When the test was unexpected and came 1 minute later, 11 of 17 did.
- After 1 hour, 6 of 17 did.

So many dogs remembered an action they had not been paying attention to for the sake of a test, but the memory faded quickly.
In an earlier study by the same group, where dogs did expect the test, about 83% still copied after an hour, against 35% here.
A memory that fades faster when it was not deliberately stored is what the authors expected of episodic-like memory.

### Remembering what they did themselves

The same group trained [10 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-020-67302-0) to repeat their own last action on the command "Repeat!".
The owners then gave the command unexpectedly, after the dog had done something on its own, such as jumping on the sofa or drinking from its bowl.

- Without a delay, and after 20 seconds, 7 of 10 dogs repeated what they had just done.
- After 1 hour, only 3 or 4 of the 10 did.

A lab in the United States has published a similar study, which would be an independent replication, but I could not read its funding statement, so I don't cite it.

### Remembering where they had eaten

A study in Kyoto used no training at all.
[Twelve pet dogs](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-012-0529-3) were led on the lead past four bowls, each with a treat in it, and allowed to eat from only two.
The owners then took their belongings and left as if going home, for a walk of 12 to 18 minutes.
Meanwhile, the bowls were swapped for an identical set with no food in them, so the dogs could not simply follow the smell.

When the dogs were brought back unexpectedly and let off the lead, 11 of 12 went first to a bowl whose treat they had not been allowed to eat.
In a second experiment with 39 dogs in Kyoto and Berlin, the dogs again went to the right bowl more often than chance, but the result was much clearer in the Japanese dogs.
Nearly half of the German dogs went back to the bowl they had already emptied.
The authors could not explain the difference.

### Remembering what, where and when, by smell

In Ontario, [16 pet dogs](https://doi.org/10.1037/com0000174) were walked past four boxes, each with a different smell, in a set order.
They were then shown two of the boxes and rewarded for choosing the one they had visited earlier.
The dogs learned to do this, and could still do it when the test was changed in ways they could not predict.

This is evidence that dogs can keep track of what they smelled, where, and in which order.
But the dogs were trained over many sessions, and the authors say their results do not show whether the three pieces formed one memory of the event.

## How solid is this?

**Fairly solid:** dogs can remember a single, unremarkable event that they had no reason to memorise, at least for an hour.
Three research groups, in Hungary, Japan and Canada, found this with different methods.

**Still uncertain:**

- **Small samples.** The studies tested between 10 and 39 dogs each, and the hour-long results rest on a handful of dogs: 6 of 17 in one study, 3 or 4 of 10 in the other.
- **Special dogs.** The Budapest dogs were trained to imitate and repeat, which most pet dogs are not.
- **Short delays.** The longest delay tested for an unexpected memory of a one-off event was about an hour.
  I found no study that tested unexpected recall of a single event after days.
- **Unexplained differences.** The Kyoto and Berlin dogs behaved differently in the same test.
- **One funding gap.** For the Ontario study, I could read only the master's thesis it came from, which has no funding statement and no industry author; I could not read the journal paper's own statement.

## So do dogs live in the moment?

Not in the sense of having no past.
Dogs carry information about specific events forward in time, sometimes for a long time.
A few unusually gifted dogs that had learned the names of 12 new toys still [knew some of the names 2 years later](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2024.0208), without practice in between; 4 of the 5 dogs tested remembered between 3 and 9 of them.
Those dogs are rare, and most family dogs never learn toy names at all, so this shows what a dog's memory can do rather than what a typical dog does.

What no study can show is whether a dog _experiences_ its past as we do: replaying a walk in its mind, or thinking about yesterday while lying on the sofa.
The honest answer is that dogs clearly remember, and we don't know what remembering is like for them.

[How long dogs hold things in memory](/faq/how-long-is-a-dogs-memory) and [whether they have a sense of time](/faq/do-dogs-have-a-sense-of-time) are separate questions with their own evidence.

## Do dogs remember people they met only once?

Probably not as individuals, according to the one study I found.
In Budapest, [20 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-022-01624-6) played with a stranger who helped them get a ball out of a cage.
A week later, the dogs were offered that person and another stranger.

- The dogs did not go to the familiar person first; their choices were at chance.
- But when they faced the ball task again, they looked to the person for help sooner than they had the first time.

The same happened with 47 other dogs that had met a small helpful robot instead, whether they were tested a day, a week or a month later.
So the dogs seemed to remember _what had happened_, someone helping them, but not _who_ had done it.
The authors conclude that dogs need more time with a person before they remember that person specifically.

This is one study from one lab, and a choice test in a lab may miss subtler signs of recognition.
I found no independent study of how long dogs remember people they know well, such as a former owner, after months or years apart.
