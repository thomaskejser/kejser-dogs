Usually one to three.
In every set of breeding records I found, most females had one or two litters, and only a few had more than four.

The rules allow more than that.
The UK's Royal Kennel Club allows four litters, the Swedish Kennel Club five, and English law six for licensed breeders, while US federal law and Germany's dog-keeping law set no limit at all.

## What the records show

- **Drevers in Sweden, 1995–2006.**
  In the Swedish Kennel Club's register, [1,665 bitches](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2008.04.051) had 2,717 litters, about 1.6 each.
  Of those litters, 1,416 were a bitch's first, 669 her second, 326 her third, 189 her fourth and only 117 her fifth or later.
  A professional kennel in the same study had 224 litters from 113 bitches, about two each.
- **Bernese mountain dogs in Sweden, 2010–2020.**
  The breed club's records covered [502 bitches](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-022-00646-x) with up to five litters each, and only eight had more than four.
  More than half of all litters were first litters, and no bitch was older than 7 when she gave birth.
- **Breeders in Germany, Austria and Switzerland.**
  In a survey covering 423 bitches of 80 breeds, each had [2.1 litters](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2022.934273) on average, with a range of one to seven.
- **Breeders in Australia.**
  Of 200 breeders, almost all members of a kennel club, most said their females [typically had two or three litters](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani15152302) before retiring, and that they usually bred one litter a year across all their dogs.

These numbers undercount somewhat, because a study covering a fixed span of years misses litters born before it starts or after it ends.
They also come from pedigree breeders who report to clubs, who are probably the most careful ones.

I found no study that counted how many litters females in commercial kennels or puppy farms have over their lives.
In 30 US commercial kennels that volunteered for a study, the adult breeding dogs averaged [3.6 years old](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0282459), with a range of 2 to 10, but that tells us their age, not their number of litters.

## Age at first and last litter

Most bitches have their first litter at two to five years old.
Among the Bernese mountain dogs, the average age at a first litter was [about three years](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-022-00646-x).
Among the Drevers, most first litters in the register came at [two to five years](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2008.04.051), and about one in five came after 5.

Few bitches give birth after 7 or 8.
In the German-speaking survey, mothers were [1.1 to 10.6 years old](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2022.934273) at whelping, with an average of 4.3.

None of the studies reported the usual gap between litters.
Bitches typically come into heat [every five to twelve months](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2023.8213), so a bitch bred on every heat can have up to about two litters a year.

## The rules

**Kennel clubs**

- **UK:** the Royal Kennel Club's [welfare standard](https://www.royalkennelclub.com/about-us/about-the-kennel-club/the-kennel-club-codes/welfare-standard) allows no mating under 12 months, no breeding over 8, at most one litter a year, at most four litters in total, and none after two caesareans.
- **Sweden:** the Swedish Kennel Club's [basic rules](https://www.skk.se/globalassets/globala-filer/regler/grundregler-for-skk-s7.pdf) allow no mating before 18 months or after 10 years, at most five litters, and a year's rest after two litters within 12 months.
  A bitch of 7 or older needs a vet's certificate, may not have her first litter at that age, and must rest a year between litters.
- **FCI**, the international federation, sets [no numbers](https://www.fci.be/medias/ELE-REG-STR-en-451.pdf) and leaves limits to its national members.
  It does say a bitch that can't give birth naturally should be retired from breeding.
- **USA:** the American Kennel Club registers litters from dams aged [8 months to 12 years](https://www.apps.akc.org/apps/litters/support/support_glossary.cfm) at mating and questions litters born less than about four and a half months apart, but sets no limit on the number.

I checked only the Swedish rules among the Nordic kennel clubs.

**Laws**

- **England:** licensed breeders must not mate a bitch [under 12 months](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/uksi/2018/486/schedule/6), let her have more than one litter in 12 months or more than six in total, or mate her after two caesareans.
- **Sweden:** the law bans mating [before 18 months](https://jordbruksverket.se/download/18.603f70b719cd5b3b9411390/1773225497353/2020-008.pdf), requires a year's rest after two litters within 12 months unless a vet approves a shorter one, and bans breeding after two caesareans.
- **Germany:** the [Tierschutz-Hundeverordnung](https://www.gesetze-im-internet.de/tierschhuv/BJNR083800001.html) sets no limit on the number of litters or the gap between them.
  For commercial breeders, it requires one carer for every five breeding dogs and their puppies.
- **USA:** the federal [Animal Welfare Act rules](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-9/chapter-I/subchapter-A/part-3/subpart-A) for licensed breeders set [no maximum](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0282459) number of litters and no retirement age, so retirement is left to the breeder.

## Health effects on the mother

**What the studies found**

- **Older mothers have smaller litters.**
  In the Drever register, litters had fewer registered puppies once the bitch was [over 5](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.theriogenology.2008.04.051), and first litters were smaller when she was over 4.
- **Older mothers need more caesareans.**
  Among the Bernese mountain dogs, bitches of 6 or older had [4.8 times the odds](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-022-00646-x) of a caesarean of bitches aged 1 to 2.
  In the German-speaking survey, emergency caesareans in first litters rose from [12% in bitches under 2](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2022.934273) to 50% in bitches aged 6 to 8.
- **Experienced mothers needed fewer caesareans.**
  Among the Bernese mountain dogs, a fourth or later litter had [0.15 times the odds](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-022-00646-x) of a caesarean of a first litter.
  This may not mean that litters protect the mother: breeders probably keep breeding bitches that whelp easily, and stop with those that don't.
- **One caesarean predicts another.**
  In the German-speaking survey, a bitch with an earlier caesarean was [4.5 times as likely](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2022.934273) to need another.
  In the Bernese mountain dogs, the difference was [not significant](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-022-00646-x).

Flat-faced breeds need far more caesareans than other dogs; see [flat-faced dogs](/faq/do-flat-faced-dogs-live-shorter-lives).
[Litter size and puppy survival](/faq/how-many-puppies-are-in-a-litter) are a separate question.

**What nobody has tested**

I found no study comparing the health of bitches with many litters against bitches with few, and none on closely spaced litters.
In 2023, the European Food Safety Authority reviewed the evidence for the EU and found [no studies](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2023.8213) showing that frequent pregnancies exhaust a bitch, and none supporting a minimum gap of 12 months between litters.
It also found that the benefits and harms of **back-to-back breeding**, mating a bitch at every heat to get three or four litters in a row, have not been studied.

**Advice, not evidence**

- Some breeders favour back-to-back breeding, arguing that the hormones of a heat without a pregnancy prime the womb for infection (pyometra), so empty heats do more harm than pregnancies.
- Kennel clubs and lawmakers favour rest between litters, on the view that frequent pregnancies exhaust the mother.
- EFSA recommends not breeding a bitch before she is fully grown, checking her condition before each mating, and a vet's check before breeding her after 8.

Each side has a plausible reason, and I found no data that settles it.
If you want to remove pyometra risk altogether, [spaying](/faq/does-neutering-help-my-dog-live-longer) does that once a bitch has finished breeding.

## Welfare in commercial kennels

The direct evidence is thin, and it comes mostly from one US research group whose studies were funded by a charitable foundation.

- **Fear.**
  Of 590 adult dogs in 30 commercial kennels, most were [physically healthy](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0282459), but 24% to 34% responded fearfully as a stranger approached, opened the pen and reached in, and 5% showed repetitive behaviour such as circling or pacing.
  Dogs in kennels with fewer dogs per carer were healthier and more sociable.
- **Pregnancy and nursing.**
  In 74 mothers from eight commercial kennels, stress, immune and health measures [changed little](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani12202820) from six weeks before birth to eight weeks after, beyond what pregnancy and nursing would explain.

The kennels volunteered for these studies, so they are probably better than average, and their results say nothing about illegal puppy farms.
The best-known study of former breeding dogs from large commercial kennels, a 2011 survey of their new owners, is behind a paywall, and I couldn't check its funding, so I have left it out.
