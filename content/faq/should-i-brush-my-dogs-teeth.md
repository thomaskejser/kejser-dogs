Yes, if your dog will let you.
Gum disease (**periodontal disease**) is the most common disease in dogs' mouths, and brushing is the home care with the best evidence behind it.

Plaque, a film of bacteria, builds up on the teeth and inflames the gums (**gingivitis**).
Gingivitis can be reversed, but left alone it can become **periodontitis**: the tissue and bone holding the teeth are lost, which [cannot easily be undone](https://doi.org/10.5326/JAAHA-MS-6933) and can end in lost teeth.

## How common is gum disease?

- **Very common.** In a random sample of [22,333 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsap.13405) seen by UK vets, 12.5% were diagnosed with periodontal disease in a single year.
- **More common than vets record.** Periodontal disease is [generally underdiagnosed](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tvjl.2021.105717), and [studies that examine every tooth](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-019-1923-8) under anaesthesia find far more: 98% of Yorkshire terriers had early periodontitis in at least one tooth by 37 weeks of age.

## Which dogs are at higher risk?

- **Small dogs.** Extra-small breeds under 6.5 kg were [up to five times more likely](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tvjl.2021.105717) to be diagnosed than giant breeds, in over three million US vet records.
  In the UK, risk [fell steadily](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsap.13405) as adult body weight rose.
- **Some breeds.** Toy Poodles, King Charles Spaniels, Greyhounds and Cavalier King Charles Spaniels had the highest odds in the UK study, and flat-faced breeds were also at higher risk.
- **Older dogs.** Risk rises with age, and in Yorkshire terriers the odds of early periodontitis [nearly tripled](https://doi.org/10.1186/s12917-019-1923-8) between 37 and 78 weeks of age.
- **Overweight dogs**, and dogs that have gone [longer since their last professional cleaning](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tvjl.2021.105717).

## How often should I brush?

Daily is best.
Every other day helps too, but may not be enough on its own.

- In a [controlled trial in Beagles](https://doi.org/10.1177/089875641503200102), daily brushing and brushing every other day both worked significantly better than brushing weekly or every other week.
  The more often the teeth were brushed, the less plaque and tartar built up, and the more existing gingivitis improved.
- In an older study, brushing every other day [did not keep the gums healthy](https://doi.org/10.1177/089875649601300405) on its own, but adding a daily dental chew helped.
- Veterinary [dental guidelines](https://doi.org/10.5326/JAAHA-MS-6933) say brushing needs to be daily to be of benefit.

These trials were small and short, lasting weeks, and mostly used dogs whose teeth had been cleaned first.
No study shows how brushing frequency changes tooth loss over a dog's life.

Daily brushing is hard to keep up.
Among owners whose dogs had been treated for periodontitis and shown how to brush, [only about 42%](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-023-00718-6) brushed daily; uncooperative dogs and trouble building a routine were the usual reasons.

## What if I can't brush?

Other products help, though none has been shown to beat daily brushing.

| Option                   | What the evidence shows                                                                                                                                                                                                                                    |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Dental chews             | One chew a day [reduced plaque, tartar and gingivitis](https://doi.org/10.1177/089875640502200102) over four weeks compared with no chew.                                                                                                                  |
| Dental diets             | A dry food designed for oral care [worked as well as](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1748-5827.1997.tb03452.x) a standard dry food plus a daily dental chew; the study had no untreated group.                                                                  |
| Water additives          | A water additive [reduced plaque and tartar](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2023.1241197) over 30 days in 40 dogs whose teeth had just been cleaned.                                                                                                        |
| Without a cleaning first | In dogs that had _not_ had a professional cleaning first, brushing or a daily chew [reduced deposits](https://doi.org/10.1177/08987564241292769) over 8 weeks; gum health improved only when a water additive was combined with a chew or weekly brushing. |

Most of these trials are small, last weeks rather than years, and were run or funded by the companies that make the products.

### Look for the VOHC seal

The Veterinary Oral Health Council (VOHC) lists products whose makers have shown, to preset standards, that they slow the build-up of plaque or tartar.
Veterinary [guidelines](https://doi.org/10.5326/JAAHA-MS-6933) recommend choosing from that list, and advise against hard chews such as antlers and bones, which can break teeth.

## How to brush your dog's teeth

- **Use toothpaste made for dogs, never your own.**
  Some human toothpastes [contain xylitol](https://doi.org/10.2147/CCIDE.S55761), which is [toxic to dogs](/faq/is-xylitol-toxic).
  Human toothpaste also contains fluoride, and in children [dental products are a major source](https://doi.org/10.1177/00220345900690S108) of swallowed fluoride; many contain enough to reach a toxic dose for a young child.
  We found no study of fluoride toothpaste in dogs, but a dog swallows the toothpaste rather than spitting it out, so it is not worth the risk.
- **Start slowly.** Let your dog get used to the taste of the toothpaste and to having its mouth handled before you use a brush; this is common advice rather than something trials have tested.
- **Be gentle with sore gums.** Brushing already inflamed gums [hurts](https://doi.org/10.5326/JAAHA-MS-6933) and can put your dog off; have your vet check the mouth first.
- **Brushing removes plaque, not tartar.** Hard, brown tartar has to be removed by a vet.

## Does gum disease affect the rest of the body?

Possibly, but this is not proven.

- In about [59,000 dogs](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.234.4.486) with periodontal disease and as many without, more severe gum disease was linked to a higher risk of later heart problems, such as endocarditis and heart muscle disease.
- In a similar study of about [165,000 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2011.01.011) with periodontal disease, compared with dogs without it, more severe gum disease was linked to a higher risk of chronic kidney disease.

Both studies used vet records, so they show a link, not that gum disease causes these diseases.
Veterinary [guidelines](https://doi.org/10.5326/JAAHA-MS-6933) note that a direct link between mouth bacteria and disease elsewhere is difficult to prove.
No study shows that brushing prevents heart or kidney disease in dogs.

## When does my dog need a professional cleaning?

Brushing does not replace a vet's check.
Even with daily brushing, dogs [still need](https://doi.org/10.5326/JAAHA-MS-6933) dental exams, X-rays and treatment under general anaesthesia from time to time.

Ask your vet to check your dog's mouth at routine visits, and sooner if you see hard tartar on the teeth or red, swollen gums.

A proper cleaning needs general anaesthesia, because the vet has to clean below the gum line, probe the gums and take X-rays.
Cleaning only the visible surfaces is [purely cosmetic](https://doi.org/10.5326/JAAHA-MS-6933).
"Anaesthesia-free" cleanings, sometimes offered by groomers, are not recommended: in a study of 46 dogs, those cleaned without anaesthesia [showed no benefit](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.25.06.0405) on a saliva test for gum disease, while dogs cleaned under anaesthesia improved.
