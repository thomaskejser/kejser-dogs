Probably, if your dog will let you.
Gum disease (**periodontal disease**) is very common in dogs, and brushing is the home care vets usually recommend.
But the evidence that brushing works, and how often it is needed, is weaker than I expected once industry-funded studies are left out.

Plaque, a film of bacteria, builds up on the teeth and inflames the gums (**gingivitis**).
Left alone, gingivitis can become **periodontitis**: the tissue and bone holding the teeth are lost.
In 2,201 small and medium-sized dogs at Korean dental hospitals, periodontal disease was the reason for [82% of the teeth extracted](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani15020224).

## How common is gum disease?

- **Very common.** In 184 dogs in Mexico that had never had a dental cleaning, [79% had gingivitis](https://doi.org/10.1155/vmi/6628061) and 30% had periodontal disease.
- These figures come from one city, and [other studies report](https://doi.org/10.1155/vmi/6628061) rates from about 10% to nearly all dogs, depending on the dogs studied and how closely the teeth are examined.

## Which dogs are at higher risk?

- **Older dogs.** In the Mexican study, about half the dogs over 7 years old had periodontal disease, against about one in eight of those aged 1 to 3.
- **Small dogs.** The same study found periodontal disease more often in small dogs, and in the Korean study periodontal disease was the main reason for extractions [particularly in older dogs and small breeds](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani15020224).

The larger studies of breed, body weight and other risk factors that I found were funded by pet-food companies or a pet insurer, which I don't cite.

## How often should I brush?

Vets usually [advise brushing every day](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-023-00718-6), but I could not find an independent trial that tested this.
The trials I found that compared brushing frequencies were run by employees of companies that make dental chews or pet food, which I don't cite.

- In a small Korean study of 19 dogs, those brushed at least weekly had [slightly less tartar and gum inflammation](https://doi.org/10.3390/vetsci11020096) than those brushed less than once a month, but the difference was not significant.
  None of the owners brushed every day.

So daily brushing is a reasonable default, but I cannot say from independent evidence how much it helps or whether brushing less often is enough.

Daily brushing is also hard to keep up.
Among owners whose dogs had been treated for periodontitis and shown how to brush, [only about 42%](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-023-00718-6) brushed daily; uncooperative dogs and trouble building a routine were the usual reasons.

## What if I can't brush?

Dental chews, dental diets and water additives are sold to slow plaque and tartar.
I could not find an independent study of any of them; the trials I found were funded or run by the companies that make these products, which I don't cite.

## How to brush your dog's teeth

- **Use toothpaste made for dogs, never your own.**
  Some human toothpastes [contain xylitol](https://doi.org/10.2147/CCIDE.S55761), which is [toxic to dogs](/faq/is-xylitol-toxic).
  Human toothpaste also contains fluoride, and in children [dental products are a major source](https://doi.org/10.1177/00220345900690S108) of swallowed fluoride; many contain enough to reach a toxic dose for a young child.
  I found no study of fluoride toothpaste in dogs, but a dog swallows the toothpaste rather than spitting it out, so it is not worth the risk.
- **Start slowly.** Let your dog get used to the taste of the toothpaste and to having its mouth handled before you use a brush; this is common advice rather than something trials have tested.
- **Brushing removes plaque, not tartar.** Hard, brown tartar has to be removed by a vet.

## Does gum disease affect the rest of the body?

Possibly, but this is not proven.

- In 131 dogs at an Iranian university hospital, those with periodontal disease had [higher blood markers of inflammation](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.vas.2026.100882), such as C-reactive protein, than healthy dogs, and the markers rose with the amount of plaque and gum inflammation.

That study looked at each dog once, so it shows a link, not that gum disease causes inflammation elsewhere.
The large studies linking gum disease to heart and kidney disease were funded by a company owned by a pet-food maker, which I don't cite.
No study shows that brushing prevents heart or kidney disease in dogs.

## When does my dog need a professional cleaning?

Ask your vet to check your dog's mouth at routine visits, and sooner if you see hard tartar on the teeth or red, swollen gums.

A proper cleaning is done under general anaesthesia.
"Anaesthesia-free" cleanings, sometimes offered by groomers, are not a substitute: in a study of 46 dogs, those cleaned without anaesthesia [showed no benefit](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.25.06.0405) on a saliva test for gum disease, while dogs cleaned under anaesthesia improved.
