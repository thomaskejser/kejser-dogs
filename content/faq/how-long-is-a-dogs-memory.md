It depends on what kind of memory you mean, but in every case it is much longer than the two minutes often quoted.
**Short-term memory**, holding something in mind right now, such as where a treat was just hidden, lasts at least tens of seconds and fades over minutes.
**Long-term memory**, of trained actions and learned names, can last hours, days and, in a few unusual dogs, years.

The evidence is uneven.
Most studies test fewer than 40 dogs, and the one large study is run by owners at home.
I say below which findings are solid and which rest on one small study.

## Where the "2-minute memory" comes from

In 2015, three Swedish researchers pooled more than 90 data sets from one kind of lab test, **delayed matching-to-sample**, across 25 species of birds, mammals and bees.
In that test an animal sees a picture or light, it is taken away, and after a delay the animal must pick the matching one from two or more choices.
The review concluded that in this task [memory spans range from a few seconds to several minutes](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beproc.2014.11.019).

The press turned that into a headline about dogs.
National Geographic reported that ["dogs forget an event within two minutes"](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/animals/article/150225-dogs-memories-animals-chimpanzees-science-mind-psychology), and gave 27 seconds as an average across the animals studied.
Those numbers have been repeated ever since as "dogs have a 2-minute memory" or "a 27-second memory".

The review itself says something narrower.
It measured memory for arbitrary lab stimuli that mean nothing to the animal.
The authors wrote that much longer memories, of days to months, exist for **biologically meaningful information**, such as where food is.
So the review does not claim that dogs forget everything after two minutes, and the studies below show they don't.

## Short-term memory: seconds to minutes

**Solid:** dogs remember where food was hidden for at least 40 seconds, and accuracy falls as the delay grows towards minutes.

- The Dog Aging Project had owners run two simple games at home with [6,753 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11357-025-01867-4).
  - In "Treat Hide & Seek" (3,775 dogs), a treat was hidden in one of two boxes, and the dog was released after 0, 10, 20 or 40 seconds.
    Dogs chose the right box on 92% of trials, far above the 50% expected by chance.
  - In the owners' data, accuracy did not fall over 40 seconds; it was even slightly better at the longer delays.
    The longer delays always came later in the session, so practice may have hidden some forgetting, and most dogs made no errors at all, so the game was too easy to show a limit.
  - In 30 dogs tested by researchers with the same games, scores were similar overall, and there was a trend towards worse performance at the longer delays.
- In a lab-style test in owners' homes, [41 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-024-01845-x) watched a toy hidden behind one of four boxes, then waited 0, 1 or 2 minutes behind a screen.
  Their chance of finding it fell as the wait grew longer.
  Dogs with painful arthritis declined faster than healthy dogs.
- A study of [26 pet dogs](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-020-01447-3) with a two-box task likewise found that longer delays led to more mistakes.

So a dog's working memory for "where is the treat" fades over a few minutes, like that of other animals.
A classic study that tested delays of up to 4 minutes is often cited, but I could not read its funding statement, so I don't use it here.

## Long-term memory: trained actions

**Suggestive:** dogs remember a trained action they watched a person do for at least a day.

- [12 pet dogs](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-015-0931-8) trained in "Do as I do", a game where the dog copies an action its owner shows it, watched an action and were asked to copy it 1 to 24 hours later.
  They did as well as 12 dogs asked to copy it straight away, with no sign of forgetting over 24 hours.

The dogs were experienced at the game, and the study is small and from one lab.
I could not find a study I can cite on how long ordinary pet dogs keep everyday training, such as "sit" or "come", without practice.
Owners' experience suggests it lasts a long time, but that is not the same as evidence.

## Long-term memory: names of toys

Some dogs learn the names of dozens or hundreds of toys.
These **gifted word learner** dogs are rare, and the findings do not apply to most dogs.

- **Rico**, a border collie, knew the names of more than 200 items.
  In a [famous 2004 study](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1097859), he learned a new toy's name by working out that an unfamiliar word must mean the one unfamiliar toy.
  Four weeks later he fetched the right toy in only 3 of 6 sessions, which the authors reported as P < 0.1, short of the usual 5% standard for statistical significance.
  Ten minutes after learning, he managed 4 of 6.
  This was one dog, and a later group argued that such early studies [lacked proper control tests](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-81699-2).
- In that later study, [2 gifted dogs](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-021-81699-2) heard their owner name a new toy 4 times while playing.
  They remembered the name for at least 2 minutes, but memory tended to fade after 10 minutes and 1 hour, which suggests one short lesson is not enough to make it stick.
  The same procedure with 20 typical family dogs taught them nothing: none showed any sign of learning the names.
- [5 gifted dogs](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsbl.2024.0208) were tested on 12 toys whose names they had learned 2 years earlier, with no practice in between.
  As a group they chose correctly 44% of the time, against about 20% by chance.
  4 of the 5 dogs did better than chance, each retrieving between 3 and 9 of the toys at least once.
  They had been tested on the same toys 1 and 2 months after learning them, which may have helped.

So a few exceptional dogs keep some word memories for years.
Do not expect a typical dog to learn toy names this way.

## Memory gets worse with age

**Solid:** short-term memory declines measurably in healthy older dogs, starting in middle age.

- In the Dog Aging Project, accuracy on a three-box game fell from about 72% at age 3 to about 62% at age 13, and on Treat Hide & Seek from about 93% to about 88%.
  The decline [began in midlife](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11357-025-01867-4) and was the same in small and large dogs, even though large dogs age faster in other ways.
- In the 26-dog study, performance [rose until middle age and then declined](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10071-020-01447-3), with both young and old dogs doing worse.
- [18 old dogs](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-19918-7) (about 12 years old) made more errors than 20 young ones (about 3 years old) in a memory test, and the difference held when they were retested two weeks later.
- In [90 healthy pet dogs](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bbr.2022.113762), older dogs did worse at remembering a past event they had not been told to remember, regardless of sex or size.

These studies all compare young and old dogs at one point in time rather than following the same dogs as they age, so they show an association with age, not how fast an individual dog declines.
The decline is mild in most dogs; a minority develop **canine cognitive dysfunction**, a dementia-like condition.
Whether exercise helps is covered in [Does exercise help keep an older dog's mind sharp?](/faq/does-exercise-help-an-older-dogs-mind).

Whether dogs [remember particular past events](/faq/do-dogs-remember-past-events), and whether they [sense how much time has passed](/faq/do-dogs-have-a-sense-of-time), are separate questions.
