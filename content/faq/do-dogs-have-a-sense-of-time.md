Partly.
Dogs can time short intervals of a few seconds, and they behave differently after a long absence than after a short one.
But I found very little research on how dogs experience hours, and none on whether they know when it is time for dinner or a walk.

## Can dogs time seconds?

Yes, in the lab.
Two small studies trained dogs to report whether a signal was "short" or "long", then tested them on durations in between.

- In New Zealand, six companion dogs learned to press one lever after a short light and another after a long one.
  The pairs ranged from 0.5 versus 2 seconds up to 4 versus 16 seconds.
  The dogs [sorted the durations correctly](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani9100801), and their switch point fell near the geometric mean of each pair (about 2 seconds for 1 versus 4), as it does in rats and pigeons.
  Their precision was not quite constant across the ranges, as one classic rule of timing predicts.
- In Brazil, five dogs learned to pick one colour after a 1-second tone and another after a 4-second tone.
  On tones of 1.4, 2 and 2.8 seconds, they [chose "long" more often the longer the tone](https://doi.org/10.1037/pne0000080), and the switch point was slightly below 2 seconds.

Two labs found the same pattern, which makes it more convincing, but 11 dogs in total is a very small sample.
Both studies tested durations of 16 seconds or less, so they say nothing about minutes or hours.

## Do dogs notice how long they were left alone?

There is one study, and it suggests they notice something.

In Sweden, 12 pet dogs used to being home alone were filmed on three days, when their owners left them for 30 minutes, 2 hours and 4 hours, in random order.

- **While alone,** the dogs mostly lay resting, and their behaviour and heart rate [did not differ](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2010.11.015) between the three absences.
- **At reunion,** dogs left for 2 or 4 hours wagged their tails about three times as often, and started about three times as many interactions with their owner, as dogs left for 30 minutes.
  They were also more active, and licked their lips and shook their bodies more.
- **The owners' own greetings** did not differ between the absences, so the owners were not simply greeting their dogs more after a long day.
- **Heart rate** in the first minutes after reunion tended to be higher after 2 hours than after 30 minutes, but the difference was not statistically significant.
- **2 hours and 4 hours** looked much the same on average.

The authors point out that they cannot tell whether the dogs tracked the time while alone or only reacted when the owner came back.
I would add that a dog that has rested longer, or needs to go out, might also greet its owner more eagerly.
So the study shows that dogs are affected by how long they were alone, not that they perceive hours as we do.

The same group found a [similar pattern in 14 cats](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0185599): at reunion, cats left alone for 4 hours purred and stretched more than after 30 minutes.
The owners also talked more to their cats after the longer absence, which muddies that result a little.

The dog study has never been repeated, as far as I can find, and no one has tested a full working day of 8 hours or more.

## Do dogs know when I set off home?

Many owners believe their dog knows when they start their journey home.
The only controlled test I cite studied a single dog.

British psychologists tested Jaytee, a terrier cross whose family said he waited at the porch when his owner set off home.
In four experiments, the owner set off from 5.5 to 11 miles away at a randomly chosen time, and a judge who did not know that time reviewed the video.
Jaytee's "signal" [never fell in the right time window](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1998.tb02696.x).
He went to the porch or window 4 to 13 times per session, often when a car pulled up or someone walked past.
The researchers changed the definition of a signal after the first experiment, which weakens a strict reading of the result.

One dog is not much evidence, but I found no sound study showing that dogs sense when you set off home.

## Do dogs tell time by smell?

The dog-cognition researcher Alexandra Horowitz has suggested, in her popular books, that dogs might read time from scent.
Your smell in the house fades over the day, and a dog might learn how faint it gets before you come home.

It is an interesting idea, but it is a hypothesis.
I found no study that has tested it.

## Do dogs know when it's time for dinner or a walk?

Many owners say their dog starts nagging at the same time every day.
That may well be true, but I found no peer-reviewed study of dogs anticipating meal times, walk times or an owner's usual return time.

The British Jaytee study lists learning the owner's routine as one of the ordinary explanations for a dog that seems to know when someone is coming home, but it did not test it directly.

So whether dogs anticipate clock times, or react to cues such as you moving towards the lead or the light changing, is unknown.

## What I take from this

- **Seconds:** dogs time short intervals much as other mammals do.
- **Hours:** dogs react differently after 2 hours alone than after 30 minutes, but that rests on one study of 12 dogs.
- **Everything else**, from smelling time to sensing your journey home to knowing dinner time, is anecdote, an untested idea or a single negative test.
