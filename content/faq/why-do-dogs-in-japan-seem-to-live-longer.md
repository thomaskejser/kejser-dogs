Japanese studies put a dog's life expectancy at about 14 years, against about 11 in the UK and 9 in South Korea and Portugal.
Most of that gap comes from _which_ dogs each study counted and how, not from where the dogs lived.
Part of it is probably real, but nobody has measured how much.

## The figures

Each country's figure comes from a different kind of data, and that is the first thing to look at.

| Country     | Data source                                                                     | Figure                              | What pushes it up or down                                                  |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |

## Why the source matters so much

Each kind of data leaves out different dogs.

- **Insurance.**
  Insured dogs may [live longer](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-10341-6) because their owners can afford more treatment.
  Breeds with a high risk of disease, such as flat-faced breeds, may be under-insured because premiums are higher and some insurers add special rules.
  Swedish life insurance [stopped at age 10](https://doi.org/10.1186/1751-0147-46-105), so it cannot give an average lifespan at all.
- **Pet cemeteries.**
  They count only dogs whose owners paid for burial or cremation.
  In the Tokyo data, [purebreds were over-represented](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.17-0384): 90.7% of the dogs were purebred against about 82.5% nationally, which the authors think made their figure _too low_, since crossbreds there lived longer.
- **Primary-care vet records** cover most owned dogs, including puppies that die young.
  The [UK life table](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-10341-6) comes from these.
- **Referral and teaching hospitals** see sicker dogs, and US studies from them gave averages of [8.5 to 9 years](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-020-00086-8).
- **Registries** depend on owners reporting deaths.
  In Portugal, many deaths were [reported late or never](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani14152141), and the authors had to remove "phantom" dogs that were still listed as alive at implausible ages.
  The table was also built only from dogs that died in 2013–2022, which the authors note tends to underestimate lifespan.

### Puppy deaths and late entry

Whether a study catches dogs that die young can move the figure by a year or more.

- In Seoul, [16% of dogs died in their first year](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2025.1532422), so life expectancy was 9.39 years at birth but 10.12 at age one.
- Studies that count a dog only once it turns up at a clinic during the study period miss dogs that died before then.
  This is called _left truncation_, and it pushes the average up.
  The US study with a median of 15.4 years [took dogs seen at three clinics over one year](https://doi.org/10.1186/s40575-020-00086-8), so I think it may be affected.
- The opposite bias hits breeds that have become popular quickly: most of those dogs are still young, so those that have died so far died young.
  This is one reason UK French bulldogs had a life expectancy of [only 4.53 years](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-10341-6).

### What the UK study says about Japan

The authors of the UK life table compared their 11.23 years directly with Japan's 13.7, and [gave these reasons](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-10341-6):

- the Japanese figures come from insured dogs and cemetery records;
- high-risk breeds are probably under-represented in insurance data;
- toy and small breeds, which live longer, are more common in Japan, and large and flat-faced breeds more common in the UK.

But they also found the gap _within_ breeds, comparing UK life tables with the Japanese cemetery study:

| Breed              | UK          | Japan      |
| ------------------ | ----------- | ---------- |
| Labrador Retriever | 11.77 years | 14.1 years |
| Shih Tzu           | 11.05 years | 15.0 years |
| Beagle             | 9.85 years  | 14.8 years |
| Pug                | 7.65 years  | 12.8 years |
| French Bulldog     | 4.53 years  | 10.2 years |

So breed mix cannot be the whole story.
The authors suggest the smaller Japanese samples may explain part of it.
I think the different data sources explain more.

## What is probably real

### Breed mix

Japan's dogs are mostly small.
The most common breeds in the [Tokyo cemetery study](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.17-0384) were the Miniature Dachshund, Chihuahua, Shih Tzu, Yorkshire Terrier, Shiba and Toy Poodle.
In the insured Japanese dogs, [life expectancy fell with weight](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2015.03.018), from 14.2 years at 5–10 kg to 10.6 years at 40 kg and over.

The UK has more large and flat-faced dogs.
Among 2.2 million dogs seen by UK vets in 2019, the Labrador was the most common breed after crossbreds, and [17.6% were flat-faced](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0288081) (brachycephalic) breeds.
In Portugal, [skull shape mattered more than body size](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani14152141): large flat-faced breeds lived shortest.

### Causes of death by setting

Where dogs roam and infections are common, more die young.

- In Turkey, the [most common cause of death](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rvsc.2023.104953) in an owner survey was viral infection, and dogs allowed to roam freely had a 59% higher risk of death.
  This was a self-selected online survey of 1,312 dogs.
- In insured Swedish dogs under 10, [trauma caused 17%](https://doi.org/10.1186/1751-0147-46-105) of deaths with a diagnosis, close behind tumours at 18%.
- In recent Japanese data, infection and injury hardly figure.
  In the [cemetery questionnaire](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.21-0171), injury caused 1.0% of deaths and infection 0.1%; "senility" and cancer led.
  In [vet records](https://doi.org/10.12935/jvma.75.e128), cancer, heart and urinary diseases led.

### Tokyo's rise from 8.6 to 13.7 years

The strongest evidence that Japanese dogs really do live longer is a comparison with Japan itself.
Cemetery records from Tokyo in 1981–82 gave a life expectancy of 8.3 years at birth and 8.6 at age one; the same kind of data from 2012–15 gave [13.7 years](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.17-0384).
In the early 1980s the leading causes of death were heartworm, intestinal worms, distemper, traffic accidents and malnutrition.
The authors put the rise down to more veterinary care and better food.

Even this comparison is imperfect: the older study had few young dogs, because owners then seldom buried young dogs, and owners rounded older dogs' ages.

### Indoor living

This is often given as a reason, but I found no study that measured how indoor living affects lifespan in Japan or anywhere else.
In the [cemetery questionnaire](https://doi.org/10.1292/jvms.21-0171), dogs that lived indoors were less likely to have "senility" recorded as their cause of death.
The authors think that is because owners living closely with their dogs notice diseases more often, not because indoor dogs live longer.

## What nobody has measured

No study has compared dog lifespans between countries using the same method.

- A 2024 review tried to pool lifespan studies and found the data [too inconsistent for a meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.24.01.0068).
  Studies used cemetery, clinic, registry and insurance data, started their life tables at birth or at age one, and handled euthanasia and accidental deaths differently.
  The authors concluded that, if anything, dogs' lifespans have risen since the 1980s, and called for a standard way of recording dog deaths.
- Euthanasia is how most owned dogs die, so lifespan partly reflects what owners and vets decide, and it can [make life expectancy look shorter](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-022-10341-6).
  No study has compared euthanasia practice between countries.
- The only cross-country comparison I found used the same test on dogs in two countries, but measured "biological age" from DNA, not lifespan: Korean dogs [scored as ageing faster](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2528874123) than US dogs.
  I could read only the abstract, so I cannot judge whether differences in sampling explain it.

So the honest answer is that Japanese dogs probably do live somewhat longer than dogs in many countries, mainly because so many are small breeds that are well cared for.
How much longer is unknown, and most of the headline gap is an artefact of the data.
