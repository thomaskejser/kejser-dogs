Probably, yes.
Keeping a dog lean is one of the few things within an owner's control with reasonably good evidence of a longer life.
A [2025 systematic review](https://doi.org/10.4142/jvs.25215) of 62 lifespan studies reached the same conclusion: diet restriction lengthens life, and overweight dogs die younger.

The evidence comes from two kinds of study:

- **One lifelong feeding trial** in 48 Labradors, the only study that has tested [diet against lifespan](https://doi.org/10.4142/jvs.25215) in dogs.
  It shows cause, but it is small and covers one breed living in a kennel.
- **Large studies of veterinary records** from tens of thousands to millions of pet dogs.
  They cover real pet dogs, but they show association, not cause.

Pet food companies paid for the trial and both of the large records studies.

## The Purina Labrador trial

### Design

- 48 Labrador puppies from seven litters were paired by sex and weight at six weeks old, and one dog in each pair was chosen at random to eat [25% less](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114507871686) than its partner.
- Both dogs in a pair ate the same food; only the amount differed.
- The feeding started at eight weeks of age and lasted until each dog died, in a research kennel near St Louis.
- The last dog died in 2001, at 14.5 years.
- 46 of the 48 dogs were put to sleep, under rules set in advance for the colony, and two died on their own.

The control dogs were not left to eat as much as they liked for life.
At first they ate freely, but from about three years of age their food was [held at a fixed amount](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114507871686) to stop them becoming seriously obese.
Over ages 6 to 12, the control dogs had a mean body condition score of 6.7 on the nine-point scale, which is mildly overweight, and the restricted dogs 4.6, which is ideal.
So the trial compared ideal dogs with mildly overweight dogs, not normal dogs with starved ones.

### Results

- **Lifespan:** the restricted dogs lived a [median of 13.0 years](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114507871686), against 11.2 years for the control dogs.
  When the last control dog died, 9 of the 24 restricted dogs were still alive.
- **Chronic disease:** the restricted dogs needed long-term treatment for a chronic disease at a median age of 12.0 years, against 9.9 years for the control dogs.
- **Causes of death:** the dogs in both groups [died of much the same diseases](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.2005.226.225); the restricted dogs got them later.
- **Metabolism:** the restricted dogs had [lower body fat, insulin and glucose](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.2002.220.1315).
  Across all 48 dogs, [body fat above 25%](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114507871686) went with rising insulin resistance, which in turn predicted a shorter life.

### Osteoarthritis

The clearest difference was in the joints.

- At eight years, [15 of 22 control dogs](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.2000.217.1678) had arthritis in the hips on X-ray, against 3 of 21 restricted dogs.
- Arthritis in several joints was found in [77% of control dogs and 10% of restricted dogs](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114507871686) at that age.
- Hip arthritis first showed on X-ray at a [median of 6 years](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.229.5.690) in the control dogs and 12 years in the restricted dogs.

### Limits

- **Size:** 48 dogs is a small trial, and there has been no second one.
- **One breed:** Labradors are prone to obesity and joint disease; the result may be smaller or larger in other breeds.
- **Kennel life:** the dogs lived in a research colony, not in homes.
- **Euthanasia:** nearly every dog was put to sleep, so lifespan partly reflects when people judged a dog too ill to go on.
- **Funding:** the trial was [funded by Nestlé Purina](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0007114507871686) and run at its research facility, and the lead authors worked for Purina.
  The trial compared amounts of the same food, so the result does not favour any product.

The 2025 systematic review rated the [certainty of this trial's evidence as low](https://doi.org/10.4142/jvs.25215), mainly because of its size.

## Pet dogs: the large records studies

Two studies of medical records from Banfield, a chain of US veterinary hospitals, show the same pattern in pet dogs.
Banfield, WALTHAM and Royal Canin are all owned by Mars, which also sells pet food.

### Salt 2019: overweight in middle age

- The study included [50,787 neutered dogs](https://doi.org/10.1111/jvim.15367) of 12 common breeds, aged 6.5 to 8.5 years.
- Dogs recorded as heavy at every visit between 5.5 and 9.5 years were compared with dogs always recorded as normal, matched on sex, age, year and location.
- In every breed, the overweight dogs were more likely to die at any given age.
  The increase in risk ranged from 35% in German Shepherds to 186% in Yorkshire Terriers.
- The overweight dogs died younger in every breed:

| Breed (males)      | Normal weight | Overweight | Difference |
| ------------------ | ------------- | ---------- | ---------- |
| Yorkshire Terrier  | 16.2 years    | 13.7 years | 2.5 years  |
| Dachshund          | 16.4 years    | 14.1 years | 2.3 years  |
| Beagle             | 15.2 years    | 13.2 years | 2.0 years  |
| Labrador Retriever | 13.3 years    | 12.7 years | 0.6 years  |
| German Shepherd    | 12.5 years    | 12.1 years | 0.4 years  |

The gap was largest in small breeds and smallest in large ones, and the authors could not say why.
Four of the five authors worked for WALTHAM or Banfield, and the fifth author's university post was paid for by Royal Canin.
The authors stated plainly that the study was observational and that cause cannot be assumed.

### Montoya 2023: obesity across a whole population

- Life tables were built from the records of [13.3 million dogs](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2023.1082102) seen at Banfield between 2013 and 2019.
- Each dog was grouped by its median body condition score across its records, on a five-point scale.
- Obese dogs (5 of 5, or 8 to 9 on the nine-point scale) had a life expectancy at birth of 11.7 years, against 13.2 years for dogs of ideal weight.
- Overweight dogs (4 of 5, or 6 to 7 on the nine-point scale) lived as long as ideal dogs: 13.1 years.

This suggests that most of the risk comes with real obesity rather than a few extra kilos.
It does not fit easily with Salt 2019, where "heavy" dogs did worse; Salt's heavy group may have included many obese dogs.
The study was funded by Royal Canin, and four of the seven authors worked there.

## Osteoarthritis and other diseases

Osteoarthritis is the disease most clearly tied to excess weight.

- **In the Labrador trial,** less food meant less and later arthritis, as above.
  This is the causal evidence.
- **In UK pet dogs,** of [455,557 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-018-23940-z) seen by vets in 2013, about 2.5% had a diagnosis of limb osteoarthritis.
  Dogs at or above the average weight for their breed and sex had 2.3 times the odds of the diagnosis.
  The study had only body weight, not body condition, so a heavy dog may be big-boned rather than fat.
- **At a US referral hospital,** among [40,038 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tcam.2022.100640), orthopaedic disease was one of the conditions associated with overweight and obesity.
- **Weight loss helps arthritic dogs.**
  In [14 obese dogs with hip or elbow arthritis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11259-010-9348-7) put on a diet, lameness eased measurably once they had lost about 6 to 9% of their body weight.
  The trial had no control group.
- **Quality of life improves too.**
  In [50 obese dogs](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tvjl.2011.09.015) on a weight-loss programme, owners of the 30 that reached their target reported more vitality and less pain afterwards.
  The owners knew their dogs were dieting, which may colour their answers.

Obesity is also linked with other diseases in dogs, but I have not reviewed that evidence here.

## How common overweight dogs are

It depends on who measures and how.

- **When a researcher scores every dog,** overweight is the norm.
  In five UK practices, [59% of 696 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1748-5827.2010.00933.x) were overweight or obese: 39% overweight and 20% obese.
  At a US university hospital, [41% of 40,038 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tcam.2022.100640) were overweight or obese.
- **When it depends on what vets write down,** the figures are far lower.
  In UK clinical records, only [7.1% of 22,333 dogs](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsap.13325) were recorded as overweight in a year, and the authors concluded that vets under-record it.
  In the Banfield records, only [1.5% of dogs](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2023.1082102) had a median score of obese.
- **In Sweden,** about [30% of dogs](https://doi.org/10.3390/vetsci10070447) are estimated to be overweight.
- **Risk factors** in the UK records included being neutered, middle-aged and of certain breeds, with [Pugs, Beagles and Golden Retrievers](https://doi.org/10.1111/jsap.13325) at highest risk.
  In the five UK practices, obesity was associated with [frequent treats, less exercise](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1748-5827.2010.00933.x), the owner's age and the owner's income.

A fair summary is that somewhere between a third and well over half of pet dogs carry too much fat, and many of them are never recorded as overweight.
These are surveys of dogs taken to a vet, which may not represent all dogs.

## How to judge your dog's body condition

Weight alone does not tell you whether a dog is fat, because dogs of one breed vary in size.
Vets use a **body condition score** instead.
The usual version runs from 1 (emaciated) to 9 (severely obese), and [4 to 5 counts as ideal](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2023.1082102).

The score combines looking and feeling at three places, as taught to owners in a [Swedish study](https://doi.org/10.3390/vetsci10070447):

- **Ribs:** how much fat you feel over them.
- **Waist:** whether the dog narrows behind the ribs, seen from above.
- **Belly line:** whether the belly tucks up towards the hind legs, seen from the side.

Charts that describe each score are widely available, but the Swedish study suggests that a short lesson from someone trained is what makes owners accurate.

How well it works:

- **It tracks body fat well.**
  In [23 pet dogs](https://doi.org/10.5326/0400109), the score predicted body fat measured by a body scan (DEXA) closely.
  The study was small.
- **It is less accurate across breeds.**
  In a [study of six breeds](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.rvsc.2009.07.009), breeds differed in body composition, and the authors suggested the score should be adjusted by breed.
- **Owners tend to score too low.**
  In Glasgow, [44% of owners](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.prevetmed.2011.06.010) misjudged their dog's shape, most often thinking it leaner than it was.
  In the Swedish study, owners scored their own dogs at 4.6 on average and vet staff at 5.2, but after a 5 to 10 minute lesson on the scale, owners [scored as well as the vet staff](https://doi.org/10.3390/vetsci10070447).
- **Vets should score at every visit.**
  The American Animal Hospital Association [recommends](https://doi.org/10.5326/JAAHA-MS-7232) a body condition score and a muscle condition score at every examination.

Ask your vet to show you how to score your dog, and then check it yourself every few weeks.

## What is cause and what is association

**Causal:** feeding a dog less food, so that it stays at ideal weight instead of mildly overweight, lengthened median life by about 1.8 years and delayed arthritis by years, in one trial of Labradors in a kennel.
Randomisation means that the difference came from the food, not from anything else about the dogs.

**Association only:** the link between overweight and early death in pet dogs comes from records studies, and several things other than fat could explain part of it.

- **Owners and care.** Owners who keep a dog lean may also differ in exercise, veterinary care and when they choose euthanasia; none of the studies measured this.
- **Disease causes weight change.** Some illnesses, such as an underactive thyroid, make dogs gain weight, and others make them lose it.
  In the Banfield records, thin dogs had [far shorter lives](https://doi.org/10.3389/fvets.2023.1082102) than even obese dogs, most likely because illness had made them thin.
  Salt 2019 reduced this problem by only including dogs still under veterinary care at 9.5 years.
- **Unreliable records.** Body condition was scored by many different vets over many years, on scales that changed during the studies, and was often left blank.
- **Euthanasia.** Obesity can make another disease, such as arthritis, harder to live with and tip the decision to put a dog to sleep, so part of the shorter life may come through that decision rather than through biology.
- **Industry funding.** Purina paid for the trial and Mars companies for both records studies.
  Their results agree with each other and with what is known in other species, but no independent group has repeated them.

What is missing:

- No trial has tested whether **slimming down** an already-overweight adult dog lengthens its life; the Labrador trial kept dogs lean from puppyhood.
- Salt 2019 covered only **neutered purebred** dogs of 12 breeds, and Montoya 2023 did not separate dogs by breed or neutering when it looked at body condition.
- No study has compared how **fast** or how **early** a dog gains weight.

## What to do

- Learn to score your dog's body condition and aim for 4 to 5 out of 9.
- Count treats as part of the day's food, and keep your dog active.
- If your dog is overweight, ask your vet for a weight-loss plan; even a loss of 6 to 9% can ease the pain of arthritis.
- Be most careful with breeds prone to weight gain, such as Labradors, Beagles and Pugs, and after neutering.
